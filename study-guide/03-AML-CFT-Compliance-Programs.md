## 1. Assessing AML/CFT Risk *(pp. 245-260)*

### Why Risk Assessment Matters *(pp. 245-246)*
- The **primary goal** of an AML/CFT program is to protect the organization against ML/TF and ensure full compliance with relevant laws *(p. 245)*
- Programs must be **risk-based** -- higher-risk areas require stronger controls; lower-risk areas need less attention *(p. 245)*
- The AML function may be a dedicated department, integrated into legal, or performed by staff with other compliance duties *(p. 245)*
- Regardless of size, the program must have an **enterprise-wide view** of AML/CFT efforts *(p. 245)*

### The Risk-Based Approach (RBA) *(pp. 246-248)*
- FATF and most governments prefer RBA over prescriptive ("checklist") approaches *(p. 247)*
- Three key advantages of RBA:
  - **Flexible:** Risks vary over time across jurisdictions, customers, products, and delivery channels *(p. 247)*
  - **Effective:** Companies are better equipped than legislators to assess their specific ML/TF risks *(p. 247)*
  - **Proportionate:** Allows practical, intelligent allocation of resources; minimizes adverse impact on low-risk customers *(p. 247)*
- FATF recommends considering three risk factor categories *(pp. 247-248)*:
  - **Customer risk factors** -- nonresident customers, cash-intensive businesses, complex ownership structures, bearer shares
  - **Country/geographic risk factors** -- countries with inadequate AML/CFT systems, subject to sanctions/embargos, involved in funding terrorism, high levels of corruption
  - **Product/service/delivery channel risk factors** -- private banking, anonymous transactions, payments from unknown third parties

### Maintaining an AML/CFT Risk Model *(pp. 248-249)*
- A risk-based analysis should include both **inherent risk** and **residual risk** at the country, sectoral, legal entity, and business relationship level *(p. 248)*
- Risk models should develop understanding of inherent risks in: customer base, products, delivery channels, services offered, and jurisdictions *(p. 248)*
- Identification of ML/TF risks requires expert input from **business lines, risk management, compliance, and legal units** *(p. 248)*
- New business products and services should be evaluated for ML/TF vulnerabilities **before launch** *(p. 248)*
- Risk is **dynamic** -- must be continuously managed; ML/TF risk models should be subject to **regular review** (usually annually or when new products, delivery channels, and customer types are introduced) *(p. 249)*

### Understanding AML/CFT Risk Categories *(pp. 249-250)*
- **Prohibited:** Organization will not tolerate any dealings (e.g., sanctioned countries, designated terrorist sponsors on UN/OFAC lists) *(p. 249)*
- **High Risk:** Significant risks requiring stringent controls like EDD and rigorous transaction monitoring (e.g., countries with corruption/drug trafficking reputation; PEPs; MSBs; cash-intensive businesses; correspondent banking; private banking; international wire transfers) *(p. 249)*
- **Medium Risk:** Merit additional scrutiny but do not rise to high risk (e.g., retail business accepting low-to-moderate cash) *(p. 249)*
- **Low Risk:** Baseline risk of money laundering; typically normal, expected activity *(p. 250)*

### AML/CFT Risk Scoring *(pp. 250-252)*
- Uses **numeric values** to determine risk category across geography, customer type, and products/services *(p. 250)*
- Example: Scale of 1-10 per category (1-3 = standard, 4-8 = medium, 9-10 = high) *(p. 250)*
- Three categories combined for a **composite score** (range 3-30 in simple model) *(p. 250)*
- Models can be made more complex by **weighting factors** differently (e.g., more emphasis on customer type vs. country) *(p. 250)*
- Important: Do not let a high outlier score in one element be "averaged away" by low scores in other elements *(p. 251)*
- Thresholds should ensure **high-risk relationships do not represent too large a portion** of the portfolio *(p. 251)*
- Risk scoring is **ongoing and evolving** -- must be updated for changes in products, services, distribution channels, customers, and jurisdictions *(pp. 251-252)*

### Assessing the Dynamic Risk of Customers *(pp. 252-253)*
- Risk ratings should be **reevaluated** when triggered by key factors *(p. 252)*
- A customer's actual **activity** may be the most important driver of risk rating changes *(p. 252)*
- Example: Student checking account starts low-risk but shows high volume wires to high-risk jurisdictions -- risk must be raised *(p. 252)*
- Conversely, a seemingly high-risk customer (MSB) may conduct exactly the activity declared -- may not be as risky as inherent risk suggests *(p. 252)*
- Factors triggering risk reevaluation *(pp. 252-253)*:
  - **Unusual activity** (alerts, cases, SAR filings)
  - **Law enforcement inquiries** (subpoenas)
  - **Sanctions violations**
  - **Significant volumes of unexpected activity** (e.g., domestic charity receiving large cash deposits then engaging in international transfers)
- Higher risk customers whose activity drives a higher risk rating should be subject to **EDD** to mitigate risk *(p. 253)*

### AML/CFT Risk Identification -- Three Key Risk Factors *(pp. 254-258)*

#### Customer Type *(pp. 254-255)*
- Analyze users of products and services: individuals, listed companies, private companies, joint ventures, partnerships, financial institutions *(p. 254)*
- **Public multinational corporations** tend to score **lower** risk (more publicly available information) *(p. 255)*
- **Private companies, trusts, charities, LLCs** with complex ownership score **higher** risk (difficult to identify beneficial owners) *(p. 255)*
- Risk is even higher if corporations are based in countries with **inadequate AML requirements** or strict corporate secrecy *(p. 255)*
- **High-risk customer types** identified by supervisory authorities *(p. 255)*:
  - Banks, casinos, offshore corporations/banks in tax havens, embassies
  - MSBs (currency exchange houses, money remitters, check cashers)
  - Virtual currency exchanges
  - Car, boat, airplane dealerships; used car/truck dealers
  - Professional service providers (attorneys, accountants, investment brokers)
  - Travel agencies, broker-dealers, jewel/gem/precious metals dealers
  - Import/export companies, cash-intensive businesses (restaurants, retail, parking)

#### Geographic Location/Jurisdiction *(pp. 256-257)*
- Evaluate jurisdictional risk: where customers reside, their citizenship, where corporate customers are headquartered, where they conduct business *(p. 256)*
- Risk-rating methodology must be **documented**; in larger organizations, outputs should be **formally reviewed by executive and senior management** *(p. 256)*
- Sources for identifying high-risk countries *(pp. 256-257)*:
  - **OFAC, FCA, FinCEN** lists
  - **US Department of State** *International Narcotics Control Strategy Report*
  - **Transparency International** *Corruption Perceptions Index*
  - **FATF** mutual evaluation reports on weak AML/CFT regimes
  - **HIDTAs** (High-Intensity Drug Trafficking Areas) and **HIFCAs** (High-Intensity Financial Crime Areas) in the US
- Consider: cash-based economies, politically unstable regimes, bank secrecy havens, drug production/transit regions *(p. 257)*

#### Products and Services *(pp. 257-258)*
- Review how new and existing products/services could be used for ML/TF *(p. 257)*
- **Compliance officer should be an active participant** in project teams for new products/systems *(p. 257)*
- Consider whether products: enable rapid high-volume transactions, afford anonymity, have high transaction values, allow third-party payments, have unusual complexity, require government eligibility verification *(pp. 257-258)*
- **High-risk banking functions and products** *(p. 258)*:
  - Private banking, offshore international activity, deposit-taking, wire transfer/cash management
  - Transactions where primary beneficiary is undisclosed, loan guarantee schemes
  - Travelers checks, official bank checks, money orders, foreign exchange
  - International remittances, payment services (processors, prepaid products, ACH)
  - Remote deposit capture, trade-financing, payable through accounts

### Case Example: Westpac -- Failure to Identify High-Risk Activity *(pp. 259-260)*
- In September 2020, Westpac Banking Corporation agreed to a penalty of **AUD$1.3 billion** for over **23 million breaches** of Australia's AML/CTF Act *(p. 259)*
- AUSTRAC launched legal action in November 2019 for failing to detect and report nearly **3,000 transactions** linked to child exploitation *(p. 259)*
- Senior managers were alerted in 2016 to risks of suspicious payments using LitePay; Westpac did not implement controls for **two years** *(p. 259)*
- Westpac also failed to adequately assess high-risk correspondent banking risks, leading to relationships with respondents in sanctioned and high-risk countries including the DRC, Iraq, Libya, and Zimbabwe *(p. 260)*

> **Key Exam Points: Assessing AML/CFT Risk**
> - Risk assessment is the FOUNDATION of an effective AML/CFT program -- all controls flow from it
> - Three risk factors: **Customer type, Geographic location, Products/services** *(p. 254)*
> - Four risk categories: **Prohibited, High, Medium, Low** *(pp. 249-250)*
> - Risk scoring combines all three factors into a composite score -- beware of averaging that masks a high outlier *(p. 251)*
> - Risk is DYNAMIC -- must be reevaluated based on actual customer activity, not just initial inherent risk *(p. 252)*
> - Risk model should be regularly reviewed (usually annually) and updated for changes in business, products, or environment *(p. 249)*
> - FATF prefers RBA because it is flexible, effective, and proportionate *(p. 247)*

## 2. AML/CFT Program Elements *(pp. 261-295)*

### The Four Pillars (US: Five with CDD) *(pp. 261-262)*
- Commonly referred to as the **four pillars** of an effective AML/CFT program *(p. 261)*:
  1. **Internal policies, procedures, and controls** (first line of defense)
  2. **Designated compliance function with compliance officer** (second line of defense)
  3. **Ongoing employee training program**
  4. **Independent audit function** (third line of defense)
- In the US, FinCEN established a **fifth pillar** (2016 rule): appropriate, risk-based procedures for conducting **ongoing CDD** *(p. 261)*
  - Understanding nature and purpose of customer relationships for developing risk profiles
  - Conducting ongoing monitoring to identify and report suspicious transactions
  - Maintaining and updating customer information

### A System of Internal Policies, Procedures, and Controls *(pp. 262-267)*

#### Policies *(p. 262)*
- **Clear, high-level statements** that are uniform across the entire organization
- **Set the tone from the top** -- approved by executive management and/or board of directors
- Reflect high-level responsibilities of stakeholders throughout the organization
- Should clearly indicate the organization's **risk appetite** (which risks to accept, which to decline)

#### Procedures *(p. 262)*
- **Translate policies into acceptable and workable practices**
- Established at the **operational level** (not executive)
- Much more detailed than policies
- Must be reviewed and updated regularly to reflect changes in laws, regulations, products, and organization
- Form the basis of AML/CFT **training** and compliance monitoring

#### Controls *(p. 263)*
- Internal technology or tools ensuring AML/CFT program functions as intended within **predefined parameters**
- Alert compliance department to **potential outliers and deviations** from normal policy
- Include: management reports, automated review systems, utilization of multiple reviewers
- Examples: requiring dual signatures for large transactions, account opening systems forcing entry of required information, aggregation systems detecting reportable currency transactions

#### What AML Policies, Procedures, and Controls Should Address *(pp. 263-267)*
- Identify high-risk operations (products, services, delivery channels, customers, geographic locations) *(p. 263)*
- Periodically update risk profile and tailor AML/CFT program to manage risks *(p. 263)*
- Inform board of directors and senior management of compliance initiatives, deficiencies, SARs filed, corrective actions *(p. 264)*
- Develop and maintain **metrics reporting** (transactions monitored, alerts generated, cases created, SARs filed) *(p. 264)*
- Assign clear **accountability** for duties under the AML/CFT program *(p. 264)*
- Provide for **program continuity** despite changes in management or structure *(p. 264)*
- Meet all regulatory requirements and provide for **periodic review** (at least annually) *(p. 264)*
- Implement **risk-based CDD** policies, procedures, and processes *(p. 264)*
- Provide for **dual controls and segregation of duties** *(p. 265)*
- Comply with all **recordkeeping requirements** *(p. 265)*
- Provide sufficient **monitoring systems** for timely detection and reporting (including rationale for NOT reporting) *(p. 265)*
- Establish clear **accountability lines** for staff oversight *(p. 265)*
- Establish **training requirements and standards** *(p. 265)*
- Explain importance of reporting suspicious activity, the role of the compliance officer, and **tipping off restrictions** *(p. 265)*
- Incorporate AML compliance into **job descriptions and performance reviews** *(p. 265)*
- Implement **screening programs** for hiring; disciplinary actions for noncompliance *(p. 265)*
- Develop **quality assurance testing programs** (separate from independent audit) *(p. 266)*

### The Compliance Function and Compliance Officer *(pp. 267-270)*

#### Compliance Officer Responsibilities *(pp. 267-269)*
- Must be **designated** by the organization -- a regulatory requirement in most jurisdictions *(p. 267)*
- Should have **sufficient authority and resources** to implement the program *(p. 268)*
- Should be at a **senior level** with direct access to the board of directors or senior management *(p. 268)*
- Key responsibilities:
  - **Oversee** the day-to-day AML/CFT compliance program
  - **Design and implement** AML/CFT policies, procedures, and controls
  - Ensure the program is **reasonably designed** to comply with applicable laws
  - **Monitor** for compliance with AML/CFT requirements
  - **Report** to senior management and/or board on compliance matters
  - **Coordinate** with regulators and law enforcement
  - Serve as the **point of contact** for AML/CFT questions from staff
  - Active participant in new product/system **project teams** to identify appropriate control frameworks

### Culture of Compliance *(pp. 289-295)*
- A strong **culture of compliance** must be fostered from the top down *(p. 289)*
- Compliance staff must provide **adequate and effective challenge** to business and senior management when their actions directly or indirectly undermine AML/CFT controls *(p. 290)*
- The board of directors and senior management set the **tone** for the organization *(p. 290)*
- Noncompliance should be addressed in accordance with existing **disciplinary processes** *(p. 290)*

#### NY DFS Final Rule Part 504 *(p. 292)*
- New York State Department of Financial Services (DFS) issued Final Rule Part 504 on June 30, 2016, requiring regulated organizations to maintain transaction monitoring and filtering programs (TMPs) *(p. 292)*
- Effective January 1, 2017, requires boards of directors or senior officers to make annual certifications to DFS confirming compliance with TMP requirements *(p. 292)*
- Establishes eight minimum requirements for TMPs *(p. 292)*

#### Case Example: Apical Asset Management (AAM) -- Poor Management Oversight *(pp. 294-295)*
- In July 2020, the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) revoked the capital markets services license of Apical Asset Management (AAM) *(p. 294)*
- MAS cited inadequate AML/CFT policies and procedures, deficient customer risk assessments, and severe deficiencies in the control framework *(p. 294)*
- MAS identified significant failings in AAM's financial crime controls between 2013 and 2018 *(p. 294)*
- The CEO and director were specifically reprimanded for their failings *(p. 294)*
- No independent audit of AAM's control framework was conducted *(p. 294)*

### AML/CFT Training *(pp. 270-272)*
- An **ongoing** employee training program is required *(p. 270)*
- Training should cover:
  - Internal AML/CFT policies and procedures
  - How to identify and report suspicious activity
  - The role of the compliance officer and how to escalate concerns
  - **Tipping off** restrictions and consequences
  - Red flags and typologies relevant to the employee's role
  - Regulatory requirements and consequences of noncompliance
- Training should be **tailored** to the employee's specific role and risk exposure *(p. 271)*
- Must be **regularly repeated** and documented *(p. 271)*
- Should be incorporated into **new employee onboarding** *(p. 271)*

### Independent Audit (Third Line of Defense) *(pp. 272-275)*
- Must **test the overall effectiveness** of the AML/CFT program *(p. 272)*
- Should be conducted by persons **independent** of the compliance function *(p. 273)*
- Can be performed by internal audit (if independent) or external parties *(p. 273)*
- Audit should evaluate:
  - Adequacy of policies, procedures, and controls
  - Effectiveness of employee training
  - Compliance with regulatory requirements
  - Quality of suspicious activity monitoring and reporting
  - Adequacy of the risk assessment methodology
- Findings should be reported to **senior management and/or the board** *(p. 274)*
- Distinct from quality assurance testing (which is ongoing by compliance itself) *(p. 275)*

> **Key Exam Points: AML/CFT Program Elements**
> - Know the FOUR PILLARS: (1) Policies/Procedures/Controls, (2) Compliance Function, (3) Training, (4) Independent Audit *(p. 261)*
> - In the US, a FIFTH PILLAR was added: ongoing CDD *(p. 261)*
> - Policies set the tone from the top (board/executive level); Procedures are operational-level detail; Controls are the technology/tools *(pp. 262-263)*
> - The compliance officer must have sufficient AUTHORITY and RESOURCES, with senior-level access to the board *(p. 268)*
> - Culture of compliance is driven from the TOP DOWN -- board and senior management set the tone *(pp. 289-290)*
> - Training must be ongoing, tailored to roles, and documented *(pp. 270-272)*
> - Independent audit is the THIRD line of defense and must be independent of compliance *(pp. 272-275)*
> - The AAM/MAS case illustrates the failure of management oversight -- inadequate AML/CFT policies, no independent audit, and senior management reprimanded *(pp. 294-295)*

## 3. Know Your Customer (KYC) *(pp. 296-309)*

### Customer Due Diligence (CDD) Elements *(pp. 296-298)*
- CDD is a **critical component** of the AML/CFT program -- FATF gives it particular attention *(p. 296)*
- Core CDD elements include *(pp. 296-297)*:
  - **Identifying the customer** and verifying identity using reliable, independent source documents, data, or information
  - **Identifying the beneficial owner** and taking reasonable measures to verify identity
  - **Understanding the nature and purpose** of the customer relationship
  - Conducting **ongoing monitoring** of the business relationship and transactions
  - Keeping CDD information **up-to-date and relevant**
- A sound CDD program should include seven elements: customer identification, profiles, customer acceptance, risk rating, monitoring, investigation, documentation *(pp. 297-298)*

### CDD vs. EDD vs. SDD *(pp. 298-301)*

#### Standard CDD (Customer Due Diligence) *(pp. 296-298)*
- Applied to **all customers** at account opening and throughout the relationship
- Includes customer identification, beneficial ownership identification, understanding nature/purpose of relationship, ongoing monitoring

#### Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) *(pp. 298-300)*
- Applied to **higher-risk customers** to mitigate the heightened risk *(p. 298)*
- Triggered by: high-risk customer type, high-risk geography, high-risk products, unusual activity that raises risk rating *(pp. 298-299)*
- EDD measures may include *(p. 300)*:
  - Obtaining **additional identifying information** (e.g., source of wealth, source of funds)
  - **More frequent** review and updating of customer information
  - **Increased transaction monitoring** (lower thresholds, more frequent reviews)
  - **Senior management approval** for establishing or continuing the relationship
  - **Enhanced scrutiny** of the customer's background and purpose of the relationship
- If EDD determines activity is not suspicious, update the customer record to reflect a change explaining the activity *(p. 300)*

#### Simplified Due Diligence (SDD) *(p. 300)*
- Applied where ML/TF risk is **demonstrably low**
- Allows **reduced** CDD measures (not elimination of CDD)
- May apply to: publicly listed companies on regulated exchanges, government entities, regulated financial institutions in jurisdictions with adequate AML/CFT
- Organization must still be able to **justify** the use of SDD

### High-Risk Customer EDD *(pp. 300-301)*
- **PEPs** (Politically Exposed Persons): Require strong CDD and monitoring controls; accepting corruption proceeds from PEPs constitutes money laundering in the US and may violate sanctions rules *(p. 315)*
- Must continually **review and update** customer screening and sanctions programs (updating procedures, tuning, testing screening tools, training staff) *(p. 315)*

### Account Opening and ID&V (Identification & Verification) *(pp. 301-305)*
- Financial organizations must **verify the identity** of customers before or during account opening *(p. 301)*
- Identification documents should be from **reliable, independent sources** *(p. 301)*
- For individuals: government-issued photo ID, proof of address, date of birth *(p. 302)*
- For legal entities: registration documents, proof of legal existence, beneficial ownership information *(pp. 304-305)*
- **Non-face-to-face** identification requires additional measures (e.g., IP address verification, online device tracing) *(p. 304)*

### Case Example: Danske Bank -- ID&V Failures *(pp. 306-307)*
- In 2007, Danske Bank acquired Finnish Sampo Bank and its Estonian branch *(p. 306)*
- Between 2007 and 2015, the Estonian branch expanded its nonresident portfolio; nearly all customers were offshore and shell companies with links to high-risk countries *(p. 306)*
- Over an eight-year period, approximately **US$200 billion** in transactions were processed, most later deemed suspicious by regulators *(p. 306)*
- The Estonian branch was closed in 2019 by order of the Estonian Financial Supervisory Authority (FSA) *(p. 306)*
- Group-wide AML and KYC policies were not shared with the Estonian branch, leading to fragmented and inconsistent KYC implementation *(p. 306)*
- No PEP screening was performed; incoming payments were not screened for sanctions *(p. 307)*

### Consolidated CDD *(pp. 308-309)*
- Large financial organizations should adopt an **enterprise-wide approach** to CDD *(p. 308)*
- This allows for consistency across regions and business lines *(p. 308)*
- Can be achieved through a single version of the AML/CFT program or country-specific addenda to a global program *(p. 308)*
- **A single view of customer relationships** is of paramount importance *(p. 308)*
- When minimum CDD standards of home and host countries differ, offices in host jurisdictions should apply the **higher standard** of the two *(pp. 308-309)*

> **Key Exam Points: Know Your Customer**
> - CDD = identify customer + identify beneficial owner + understand purpose + ongoing monitoring *(pp. 296-297)*
> - EDD is for HIGH-RISK customers; SDD is for DEMONSTRABLY LOW-RISK customers *(pp. 298-300)*
> - EDD measures include: additional ID info, source of wealth/funds, more frequent reviews, senior management approval *(p. 300)*
> - PEPs always require EDD -- accepting PEP corruption proceeds is ML in the US *(p. 315)*
> - Beneficial ownership identification is critical -- the AMLA/Corporate Transparency Act creates a central BO repository in the US *(p. 236)*
> - CDD is the US fifth pillar of the AML/CFT program (2016 FinCEN rule) *(p. 261)*
> - Non-face-to-face onboarding requires additional verification measures *(p. 304)*
> - The Danske Bank case illustrates the danger of fragmented KYC across branches *(pp. 306-307)*

## 4. Monitoring and Screening *(pp. 310-324)*

### Economic Sanctions *(pp. 310-312)*
- **OFAC** (Office of Foreign Assets Control) administers and enforces US economic and trade sanctions *(p. 242)*
- OFAC sanctions programs include *(p. 242)*:
  - **Country-based sanctions:** Prohibit nearly all transactions with entire countries (e.g., North Korea, Iran, Cuba)
  - **List-based sanctions:** SDN list (Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons), Consolidated Sanctions list, Foreign Sanctions Evader list
  - **Secondary sanctions:** Directed at non-US, non-sanctioned parties dealing with sanctioned parties (e.g., Iranian and Russian businesses)
  - **Sectoral sanctions:** "Surgical/smart" sanctions targeting specific sectors (energy, finance, defense) to reduce collateral damage
- **Who must comply:** All US persons (citizens, permanent residents regardless of location), all persons/entities within the US, all US-incorporated entities and their foreign branches *(p. 242)*
- OFAC requires a **license** for otherwise prohibited transactions: **general licenses** (for classes of persons) and **specific licenses** (written authorization for specific transactions) *(p. 243)*
- OFAC can impose **significant penalties** for violations *(p. 243)*
- **Non-US banks** can be fined for noncompliance if their transactions have a US connection (e.g., passing through a US bank) *(p. 244)*

#### Sanctions Categories *(p. 310)*
- **Targeted sanctions:** Aimed at specific, named individuals -- freezing of assets and travel bans
- **Sectoral sanctions:** Aimed at key sectors of an economy to prohibit specific financial dealings
- **Comprehensive sanctions:** Generally prohibit all direct and indirect import/export, trade brokering, financing

#### Sanctions Evasion Techniques *(p. 243)*
- **Stripping:** The deliberate removal of key payment-related information to evade sanctions detection

#### Case Example: BNP Paribas -- US Sanctions Violations *(pp. 243-244)*
- In 2014, OFAC reached a record **US$963 million** settlement with BNP Paribas SA (BNPP), following a **US$8.9 billion** penalty imposed for apparent violations of US sanctions regulations *(p. 243)*
- BNPP systematically concealed, removed, and obscured references to information about US-sanctioned parties in almost **4,000** financial transactions routed to or through US banks between **2005 and 2012** *(p. 243)*
- Transactions involved sanctions on **Sudan, Iran, Cuba, and Burma** *(p. 243)*
- Methods included removing references to sanctioned parties, replacing them with BNPP's name or code words, or structuring payments to hide sanctioned party involvement *(p. 243)*

### Sanctions List Screening *(pp. 313-314)*
- Screen **new accounts, existing customers, and transaction counterparties** against terrorist, criminal, and blocked-persons sanctions/watch lists *(p. 313)*
- Screening must be ongoing -- not just at account opening *(p. 313)*
- Must continually **review and update** screening procedures, tune and test screening tools, and train staff *(p. 313)*

### PEP Screening *(pp. 314-315)*
- Because PEPs are difficult to identify, strong CDD and monitoring controls are essential *(p. 314)*
- Screening should be done at **onboarding and throughout the relationship** *(p. 315)*
- Some countries' laws make it a sanctions violation to accept PEP corruption proceeds *(p. 315)*

### Know Your Employee (KYE) *(pp. 315-318)*
- Insiders can pose the **same ML threat** as customers *(p. 315)*
- A KYE program ensures the organization understands an employee's **background, conflicts of interest, and susceptibility** to ML complicity *(p. 315)*
- KYE elements include *(p. 315)*:
  - Policies, procedures, internal controls, job descriptions, levels of authority
  - Compliance with **personnel laws and regulations**
  - **Monitoring, dual control, and accountability**
  - **Codes of conduct and ethics** specifying mandatory reporting of suspicious activity to MLRO
  - Failure to comply subjects employee to **disciplinary action and possible termination**
- **Background screening** of prospective and current employees and vendors *(pp. 315-316)*:
  - Criminal history, ties to negative media
  - Verification of references, experience, education, professional qualifications
  - In Singapore (MAS): also screening against ML/TF information sources, bankruptcy searches, credit history checks *(p. 316)*
  - Screening should be **ongoing** (not just pre-employment) -- especially for promotions to high-level positions *(p. 316)*
  - Contractors and vendors should also be screened, with **anti-corruption compliance clauses** in agreements *(p. 316)*
- **Social media monitoring** of employees may also be part of KYE programs *(p. 317)*
- FDIC provides guidance on pre-employment background screening *(p. 315)*

#### Case Example: FINRA/Citigroup Global Markets Inc. *(pp. 317-318)*
- In July 2019, FINRA fined Citigroup Global Markets Inc. (CGMI) **US$1.25 million** for failing to conduct timely and adequate background checks on more than **10,000** employees over a **seven-year** period *(p. 317)*
- FINRA found that CGMI employed or associated with **three individuals** who were subject to statutory disqualification due to previous criminal convictions *(pp. 317-318)*

### Suspicious and Unusual Transaction Monitoring and Reporting *(pp. 319-321)*
- Compliance personnel may need to gather further information before deeming a transaction suspicious and filing a **SAR** *(p. 319)*
- No hard-and-fast rules for what constitutes suspicious activity -- employees should watch for activity **inconsistent with customer's source of income or regular business** *(p. 319)*
- Monitoring systems should be **risk-based** and determined by: organization size, nature of business, location, frequency/size of transactions, types and geographic locations of customers *(p. 319)*

#### Internal Reports for Monitoring *(p. 319)*
- Daily cash activity exceeding reporting threshold
- Daily cash activity **just below** threshold (possible structuring)
- Cash activity **aggregated over time** (e.g., individual transactions over a certain amount or totaling more than a certain amount over 30 days)
- Wire transfer reports/logs with amount and geographic filters
- Monetary instrument logs/reports
- Check kiting/drawing on uncollected funds with significant debit/credit flows
- Significant change reports
- New account activity reports

#### Suspicious Transaction Reporting Process *(p. 320)*
- Procedures to **identify** suspicious/unusual transactions through: employee observations, law enforcement inquiries, transaction monitoring system alerts
- **Formal evaluation** of each instance of unusual transactions
- **Documentation** of SAR reporting decision (whether or not filed)
- Procedures to **periodically notify senior management/board** of SAR filings
- **Employee training** on detecting suspicious transactions

#### Critical SAR Rules *(p. 320)*
- **Tipping off prohibition:** Most countries prohibit disclosing the filing to the subject of the report *(p. 320)*
- In the US: organization and all directors, officers, employees, and agents **may not notify** any person involved in the transaction that it has been reported *(p. 320)*
- **Confidentiality:** Illegal to reveal information that would lead to knowledge of SAR existence -- includes divulging the SAR itself AND the fact that a SAR was or was not filed *(p. 320)*
- **Safe harbor:** Most laws grant **immunity from civil liability** to the filing organization and its employees *(p. 320)*
- **Recordkeeping:** National laws dictate retention periods and types of records that must be stored *(p. 320)*

### Automated AML/CFT Solutions *(pp. 321-324)*
- Technology capabilities *(p. 321)*:
  - **Automated customer verification:** Using third-party databases to compare customer-provided information
  - **Watch list filtering:** Screening against terrorist, criminal, sanctions, and blocked-persons lists
  - **Transaction monitoring:** Scanning transactional data for potential ML activity
  - **Automation of regulatory reporting:** Filing SARs, CTRs, and other reports
  - **Case management:** Dashboard for customer KYC, transaction history, investigations, filings
  - **Audit trail:** Documenting compliance steps for auditors and supervisory authorities
- Key system capabilities to evaluate *(pp. 322-324)*:
  - Monitor transactions and identify anomalies
  - Gather and score CDD information; store CDD data
  - Advanced evaluation of suspicious transactions in context of **customer risk profile and peer group**
  - View individual alerts within broader context of **total customer activity**
  - Workflow features for case creation, collaboration, information sharing
  - Use data from core systems to update monitoring and case management
  - Store/recall **at least 12 months** of data for trend analysis *(p. 323)*
  - Manage assignment, routing, approval of suspicious activity investigations
  - Automated SAR preparation and filing
  - Standard and ad-hoc reporting on investigations and investigator productivity
  - User-friendly risk-parameter settings; tiered user-rights access
- Additional evaluation factors: ease of use, data integration, scalability, internal resource support, user satisfaction, total cost *(p. 324)*

> **Key Exam Points: Monitoring and Screening**
> - OFAC sanctions are STRICT LIABILITY -- four types: Country-based, List-based, Secondary, Sectoral *(p. 242)*
> - "Stripping" = deliberate removal of sanctioned party information from payments (BNP Paribas case) *(pp. 243-244)*
> - Screening must be ONGOING, not just at account opening -- screen new accounts, existing customers, AND counterparties *(p. 313)*
> - KYE is equally important as KYC -- insiders pose the same ML threat as customers *(p. 315)*
> - SAR confidentiality is CRITICAL: tipping off is prohibited; even confirming whether a SAR was or was not filed is illegal *(p. 320)*
> - Safe harbor provides civil liability immunity for good-faith SAR filings *(p. 320)*
> - Automated systems should store at least 12 months of data for trend analysis *(p. 323)*
> - Transaction monitoring should be risk-based, determined by organization size, business nature, transaction frequency/size, and customer geography *(p. 319)*

## 5. Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Red Flags *(pp. 325-347)*

**These lists are NOT exhaustive but are commonly tested on the CAMS exam. Red flags do not automatically mean ML/TF -- they indicate activity that may warrant additional scrutiny.** *(p. 325)*

### 5.1 Unusual Customer Behavior *(pp. 325-326)*
- Customer has an **unusual or excessively nervous** demeanor
- Customer discusses the organization's **recordkeeping or reporting requirements** with apparent intent to avoid them
- Customer **threatens an employee** to discourage required recordkeeping or reporting
- Customer is **reluctant to proceed** with a transaction after being told it must be reported
- Customer suggests **paying a gratuity** to an employee
- Customer appears to have a **hidden agenda** or behaves abnormally (e.g., declining a higher interest rate on a large balance)
- **Public official** opens account in family member's name, begins making large deposits inconsistent with known legitimate income
- **Student** uncharacteristically transfers or exchanges large sums of money
- Account shows **high velocity** of fund movement but maintains low beginning/ending daily balances
- Transaction involves **offshore organizations with names resembling** well-known legitimate financial organizations
- Transaction involves **unfamiliar countries or islands** that are difficult to locate on a map
- **Agent, attorney, or financial advisor** acts for another person without proper documentation (e.g., power of attorney)

### 5.2 Unusual Customer Identification Circumstances *(pp. 326-327)*
- Customer provides **unusual or suspicious identification** documents or declines to produce originals for verification
- Customer is **unwilling to provide personal background** information when opening an account
- Customer tries to open account **without identification**, references, or complete local address
- Customer's permanent address is **outside the organization's service area**
- Customer's home or business telephone is **disconnected**
- Customer **does not want statements** or any mail sent to them
- Customer asks many questions about **how the organization shares customer identification** information
- **Business customer** is reluctant to provide complete information about nature/purpose of business, anticipated activity, or financial statements about a related entity
- Customer provides **no record of past/present employment** on a loan application
- Customer's **IP address or online device tracing** does not match the identifying information or government ID provided during online registration

### 5.3 Unusual Cash Transactions *(pp. 327-328)*
- Customer makes a large cash deposit **without having counted the cash**
- Customer frequently **exchanges small bills for large bills**
- Customer's cash deposits contain **counterfeit, musty, or extremely dirty** bills
- Customer enters bank **with another customer** and they go to different tellers to conduct currency transactions **under the reporting threshold**
- Customer makes a large cash deposit containing **many high-denomination bills**
- Customer opens **several accounts in one or more names** and makes cash deposits under the reporting threshold
- Customer **withdraws cash in amounts under** the reporting threshold
- Customer **withdraws cash from one account and deposits it into another** account they own
- Unusual cash transactions through **night deposit boxes**, especially large sums inconsistent with business
- **Frequent deposits or withdrawals** of large amounts for no apparent business reason or for a business that generally does not generate large cash amounts
- Customer conducts large cash transactions at **different branches on the same day** or coordinates others to do so
- Customer deposits cash into **several accounts below the reporting threshold**, consolidates into one account, then wire transfers abroad
- Customer attempts to **take back a portion** of a deposit exceeding the reporting threshold after learning a CTR will be filed
- Customer conducts **several cash deposits below threshold at ATMs**
- **Corporate account** has deposits/withdrawals primarily in cash rather than checks
- Customer frequently deposits **large sums in currency straps**
- Customer frequently **purchases monetary instruments with cash** in amounts lower than the reporting threshold
- Unusual number of **foreign currency exchange** transactions
- Customer conducts forex/currency swaps **without seeming to care about margins**
- **Noncustomer** deposits cash into a customer account, which is subsequently withdrawn at a different geographic location

### 5.4 Unusual Noncash Deposits *(p. 329)*
- Customer deposits a large number of **traveler's checks** in same denominations and in sequence
- Customer deposits large numbers of **consecutively numbered money orders**
- Customer deposits **checks and/or money orders inconsistent** with the stated purpose of the account or nature of business
- Customer deposits a large number of **third-party checks**
- Deposited funds are moved quickly out of the account via **payment methods inconsistent** with the established purpose of the account

### 5.5 Unusual Wire Transfer Transactions *(pp. 329-330)*
- Wire transfers sent/received from the **same person to/from different accounts**
- Customer uses **message type (MT) 202** for a covered payment in SWIFT messaging to obscure wire transfer information
- **Nonaccount holder** sends wire transfer with numerous monetary instruments, each **under the reporting threshold**
- Incoming wire transfer includes instructions to **convert funds to cashier's checks and mail to a nonaccount holder**
- Wire transfer activity to/from **secrecy havens or high-risk locations** without apparent business reason
- Incoming wire transfer followed by immediate **purchase of monetary instruments** for payment to another party
- **Increase in international wire activity** in an account with no history of such activity or when stated business does not warrant it
- Customer frequently shifts purported **international profits by wire transfer** out of the country
- Customer receives **many small incoming wire transfers** then orders a large outgoing wire transfer to another country
- Customer deposits **bearer instruments** followed by instructions to wire funds to a third party
- Account in the name of a **currency exchange house** receives wire transfers and/or cash deposits under the reporting threshold

### 5.6 Unusual Safe Deposit Box Activity *(p. 330)*
- Customer spends an **unusual amount of time** in the safe deposit box area (possibly safekeeping large amounts of cash)
- Customer often visits safe deposit box area **immediately before making cash deposits** under the reporting threshold
- Customer rents **multiple safe deposit boxes**

### 5.7 Unusual Activity in Credit Transactions *(pp. 330-331)*
- Customer's financial statement makes representations that **do not conform to accounting principles**
- Transaction made to appear **more complicated than necessary** using nonsensical technical terms (emission rate, prime bank notes, standby commitment, arbitrage, hedge contracts)
- Customer requests loans to **offshore companies** or secured by obligations of **offshore banks**
- Customer suddenly **pays off a large problem loan** with no plausible explanation of source of funds
- Customer purchases **certificates of deposit** and uses them as collateral for a loan
- Customer **collateralizes a loan with cash deposits**
- Customer uses **cash collateral located offshore** to obtain a loan
- Customer's **loan proceeds are unexpectedly transferred offshore**

### 5.8 Unusual Commercial Account Activity *(p. 331)*
- Business customer presents financial statements **noticeably different** from those of similar businesses
- Large business presents financial statements **not prepared by an accountant**
- **Check-cashing retail business** does not make withdrawals of cash against check deposits (indicating another source of cash)
- Small business makes deposits **inconsistent with expected activity** or receives funds from other unrelated businesses
- Customer maintains an **inordinately large number of accounts** for the type of business
- Corporate account shows **little or no regular, periodic activity**
- Transaction includes circumstances that would cause a banker to **reject a loan application** due to doubts about collateral
- **Multiple high-value payments/transfers** between shell companies with no apparent legitimate business purpose
- Transacting businesses **share the same address**, provide only a registered agent's address, or raise address-related inconsistencies

### 5.9 Unusual Trade Financing Transactions *(p. 332)*
- Customer seeks trade financing with stated prices **substantially higher or lower** than market
- Customer requests payment of proceeds to an **unrelated third party**
- Customer presents **significantly amended letters of credit** without reasonable justification or changes location of payment/beneficiary just before payment
- Customer changes **place of payment** in a letter of credit to an account in a country other than the beneficiary's stated location
- Standby letter of credit used as a **bid or performance bond** without reference to an underlying project or with unusual beneficiaries
- Letter of credit **inconsistent with customer's business**
- Letter of credit covers goods in **little demand** in importer's country
- Letter of credit covers goods **rarely produced** in exporter's country
- **Documents arrive without title documents**
- Letter of credit received from a **country considered high risk** for ML
- Obvious **overpricing or underpricing** of goods and services
- Transaction structure appears **unnecessarily complex** to obscure the true nature
- Commodities shipped through one or more jurisdictions **for no apparent economic or logistical reason**
- Transaction involves **repeatedly amended or frequently extended** letters of credit
- **Shipment size inconsistent** with regular volume of the importer or exporter

### 5.10 Unusual Investment Activity *(p. 333)*
- Customer uses an investment account as a **pass-through vehicle** to wire funds offshore
- Investor seems **disinterested** in typical investment decisions (risk, commissions, fees, suitability)
- Customer wants to **liquidate a large position** through a series of small transactions
- Customer deposits cash, money orders, traveler's checks, or cashier's checks in amounts **under the reporting threshold** to fund an investment account
- Customer **cashes out annuities** during the free-look period or surrenders them early

### 5.11 Other Unusual Customer Activity *(p. 333)*
- Customer conducts an **unusually high number** of transactions over the internet or by telephone
- Customer purchases several **open-end prepaid cards** for large amounts, inconsistent with normal business
- Funds withdrawn are **not consistent with normal business/personal activity** or include transfers to suspicious international jurisdictions
- Customer uses a **personal account for business purposes**
- Customer repeatedly uses bank/branch locations **geographically distant** from home or office without sufficient business purpose

### 5.12 Unusual Employee Activity *(p. 334)*
- Employee **exaggerates credentials, background, financial ability, or resources** of a customer in bank-required reports
- Employee involved in an **excessive number of unresolved exceptions**
- Employee lives a **lavish lifestyle** that could not be supported by salary
- Employee frequently **overrides internal controls** or established approval authority or circumvents policy (e.g., **wire stripping** -- removing the name of a high-risk person from a wire)
- Employee uses **company resources** to further private interests
- Employee **facilitates transactions** where the ultimate beneficiary/counterparty identity is undisclosed
- Employee **avoids taking periodic vacations**
- Employee functions performed by **vendors or contractors** have unusual billing or payment terms

### 5.13 Unusual Activity in a Money Remitter or Currency Exchange House Setting *(pp. 334-335)*
- Customer uses money orders, traveler's checks, or funds transfers in an **unusual manner**
- **Two or more people** work together in transactions
- Transaction is **altered to avoid filing a CTR**
- Customer frequently purchases **less than US$3,000** in instruments each time (or below local recordkeeping threshold) *(p. 334)*
- Transaction is altered to avoid completing a record of **funds transfer, money order, or traveler's checks of US$3,000 or more** *(p. 335)*
- Same person uses **multiple locations** in a short time period
- **Two or more people use the same identification**
- **One person uses multiple identification documents**

### 5.14 Unusual Activity for Virtual Currency *(p. 335)*
- **Repeated receipt** of funds transfers from virtual currency exchanges is inconsistent with customer profile
- **Multiple transfers** are made to one common end user
- Transactions involving virtual currency exchanges are followed within a brief time by **funds transfers to high-risk geographies** or ATM withdrawals in high-risk geographies
- Purchase of virtual currency **quickly follows receipt** of funds transfers from unconnected third parties
- **Multiple accounts** are used to collect and funnel funds to a small number of virtual currency accounts
- **Multiple purchases** of virtual currency are at or just below US$3,000, or the local recordkeeping requirement
- **Key words** entered into the transaction could relate to the sale of suspicious products

### 5.15 Unusual Activity in an Insurance Company Setting *(pp. 336-337)*
- **Cash payments** are made on insurance policies
- Customer **overfunds** an insurance policy and then moves money out of it, despite early-withdrawal fees
- Customer uses **multiple currency equivalents** (cashier's checks, money orders) from different sources to make insurance/annuity payments
- Customer purchases products **outside normal range** of financial wealth or estate planning needs
- Customer makes an **early withdrawal of insurance bond**, disregarding applicable fees
- Customer requests **refunds during a policy's legal cancellation or free-look period**
- Policy premiums are **paid from abroad** or by a third party, especially from an offshore financial center
- Policy stipulates **periodic payment of premiums in large amounts**
- Customer changes the **named beneficiary** to a person with no clear relationship to the policyholder
- There is a **lack of concern** for significant tax or other penalties assessed when cancelling a policy
- Insurance bonds subscribed in one country are **redeemed by a business entity in another country**

### 5.16 Unusual Activity in a Broker-Dealer Setting *(pp. 337-338)*
- Customer appears to be acting as an **agent for an undisclosed principal** but is reluctant/evasive about providing information
- Customer has **multiple accounts** under a single name or multiple names, with a large number of inter-account or third-party transfers
- Customer's account has **unexplained or sudden extensive wire activity**, especially in accounts with little or no previous activity
- Customer makes a **funds deposit for a long-term investment** then shortly requests to liquidate and transfer proceeds
- Customer engages in **excessive journal entries between unrelated accounts** without apparent business purpose
- Customer requests transaction processing in a manner that **avoids typical documentation requirements**
- Customer, for no apparent reason, engages in transactions involving **penny stocks, Regulation S stocks, and bearer bonds** (which have been used in fraud and ML)
- Customer's account shows an **unexplained high level of activity** with very low levels of securities transactions

### 5.17 Unusual Real Estate Activity *(pp. 338-340)*
- Borrower/buyer submits **invalid documents** to cancel mortgage obligations or pay off loan balances
- Same notary public/authorized representative works with and/or receives payments from an **unusually high number** of borrowers
- **Certified checks, cashier's checks, or noncash item checks** are falsified and drawn against a borrower's account rather than a financial organization
- Borrower applies for a loan for a primary residence but **does not reside there**; property used as secondary/income-generating
- Borrower requests **refinancing** for primary residence but public/personal documents indicate residing elsewhere
- **Low appraisal values**, non-arms-length relationships between short-sale buyers/sellers, previous fraudulent sale attempts
- Agent of buyer/seller in a mortgage transaction is **unlicensed**
- **Past misrepresentations** by borrower in attempts to secure funding, property, refinance, or short sales
- **Improper/incomplete file documentation**, borrower reluctant to provide more information
- Apparent **resubmission of rejected loan application** with key borrower details changed (individual to company/corporation) -- possible straw-borrower
- Borrower attempts to **structure currency deposits/withdrawals** or disguise asset value for loan-modification programs
- Third-party affiliates request **fees in advance** on behalf of distressed homeowners
- Third party **solicits distressed homeowners** for purported mortgage counseling, foreclosure avoidance, or loan modification services

### 5.18 Unusual Activity for Dealers of Precious Metals and Other High-Value Items *(pp. 340-341)*
- Diamonds originate from a country with **limited production or no diamond mines**
- Trade conducted in large volumes with countries **not part of the diamond pipeline**
- Volume of purchases/imports **grossly exceeds** expected sales
- **Gold bars, coins, and loose diamonds** sold from a jewelry store (retail)
- Increase in volume of activity in a diamond dealer's account **despite significant decrease** in industry-wide volume
- **Intermediary abroad** facilitates buying/selling of diamonds between two local companies (lack of business justification)
- Payments related to **rare or unique diamonds** made outside known trading procedures
- **Single bank account used by multiple businesses**
- Single bank account has **multiple deposit handlers** (retail and wholesale)
- **Third parties** deposit funds into a single or multiple dealer's accounts
- **Financial activity inconsistent** with practices in the diamond trade
- Deposits/transfers from foreign companies followed by **immediate transfer** of similar amounts to another jurisdiction
- Open export settled by **offsetting to, and receiving payment from, a third party**
- Funds received/transferred for import/export and the ordering customer/beneficiary is an **MSB**
- Name of receiver in payment from diamond dealer is **not the exporter/supplier**

### 5.19 Unusual Activity Indicative of Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) *(pp. 341-342)*
- Payment made by **virtually any method** (cash, wire, check, bank drafts) by a **third party with no connection** to the underlying transaction
- **Structured currency deposits** to individual checking accounts with multiple daily deposits to multiple accounts at different branches on the same day
- **Discrepancies** between description of goods/commodity in invoice and actual goods shipped
- **Letters of credit amended without justification**
- **No apparent business relationship** between parties and transactions
- **Frequent transactions in round or whole dollars**
- Funds transferred into account and moved to a **high-risk country in the same amount**
- Companies operate in jurisdictions where **business purpose is not fully understood** and there are difficulties determining ownership
- **Lack of appropriate documentation** to support transactions
- **Negotiable instruments** used to fund transactions in sequential numbers and/or with missing payee information

### 5.20 Unusual Activity Indicative of Human Smuggling *(pp. 342-343)*
- **Multiple wire transfers below US$3,000** sent from various locations to a common beneficiary in a US or Mexican city **along the southwest border**
- Multiple wire transfers at **different branches** to/from US or Mexican cities along the southwest border on the same day or consecutive days
- Money flows **do not fit common remittance patterns** (e.g., wires from countries with high migrant populations directed to border cities; beneficiaries receiving wires from migrant-population countries but are not nationals of those countries)
- **Unusual currency deposits** followed by wire transfers to high migrant population countries inconsistent with expected activity (sudden increases in deposits, rapid turnover, large cash deposits from unknown sources)
- **Multiple apparently unrelated customers** send wire transfers to the same beneficiary along the southwest border, using similar transactional information (common amounts, addresses, phone numbers) with no apparent relation to recipient
- **Funnel accounts** -- customer's account receives cash deposits (often below US$10,000 threshold) in cities where customer does not reside/conduct business; funds are quickly withdrawn same day
- **Checks from possible funnel accounts** appear pre-signed with different handwriting in signature and payee fields
- Customer **not in a cash-intensive industry** frequently exchanges small denomination bills for larger denomination (preparing proceeds for bulk cash shipments)
- When customer accounts are closed for suspicious activity, **new customers begin replicating** the same activity patterns

### 5.21 Unusual Activity Indicative of Human Trafficking *(pp. 343-345)*
- Business customer does not exhibit **normal payroll expenditures** (wages, payroll taxes, social security); payroll costs nonexistent or extremely low for size of operations
- **Wages are substantially deducted** -- employer deducts large amounts for housing, food costs; employees receive only a small fraction of wages
- **Payroll checks are cashed** but majority of funds kept by employer or deposited back into employer's account
- **Frequent outbound wire transfers** with no business purpose to countries at high risk for human trafficking
- Account appears to function as a **funnel account** with cash deposits where customer does not reside/conduct business
- Multiple unrelated customers send wire transfers to the **same beneficiary** using similar transactional information
- Transactions conducted by individuals **escorted by a third party** (pretext of requiring interpreter); third party may have possession of customer's ID
- Frequent payments to **online escort services for advertising**, online classifieds, and website hosting companies
- Frequent transactions **inconsistent with expected business activity** carried out to provide sustenance to individuals (housing, lodging, vehicle rentals, large food purchases)
- Payments to **employment or student recruitment agencies** that are not licensed/registered or have labor violations
- Customer establishes account or visits branch **while escorted by a third party** -- third party may possess customer's ID
- **Common signer/custodian** used in apparently unrelated business/personal accounts; common information (address, phone, employment info) used to open multiple accounts in different names
- **Employer or employment agency serves as custodian** for accounts of foreign workers or students
- Customer exhibits **unexplained/unjustified lifestyle** not commensurate with employment; profits/deposits significantly greater than peers
- **Inflows largely received in cash**; substantial cash receipts inconsistent with line of business; extensive use of cash to purchase assets and conduct transactions
- Additional indicators (apply in tandem with others):
  - Transactional activity inconsistent with customer's **alleged employment, business, or expected activity**
  - Cash deposits or wire transfers kept **below US$3,000 or US$10,000** to avoid recordkeeping and CTR requirements

### 5.22 Unusual Activity Indicative of Potential Terrorist Financing *(pp. 345-346)*

#### Behavior Indicators *(p. 345)*
- Parties to the transaction from **countries known to support terrorist activities**
- Use of **false corporations, including shell companies**
- Inclusion of the individual on the **United Nations 1267 Sanctions list**
- **Media reports** that account holder is linked to a known terrorist organization
- **Beneficial owner** of the account is not properly identified
- Use of **nominees, trusts, family member, or third-party accounts**
- Use of **false identification**
- **Abuse of nonprofit organizations**

#### Financial Transaction Indicators *(pp. 345-346)*
- Use of funds by **nonprofit organization inconsistent** with the purpose for which it was established
- Transaction **not economically justified** considering the account holder's business or profession
- **Series of complicated transfers** from one person to another to hide source and intended use
- Transactions **inconsistent with the account's typical activity**
- **Deposits structured below reporting requirements** to avoid detection
- **Multiple cash deposits and withdrawals** with suspicious references
- **Frequent domestic and international ATM activity**
- **No business rationale or economic justification** for transactions
- Unusual cash activity in **foreign bank accounts**
- **Multiple cash deposits in small amounts** followed by a large wire transfer to another country
- Use of **multiple foreign bank accounts**

### 5.23 Unusual Activity Indicative of Cyber Criminal Activity *(pp. 346-347)*
- **Phishing Scams:** Fraudulent emails/texts purporting to be from a reputable company to obtain login credentials or personal identifiable information; can be individual or mass (bulk phishing)
- **Spear Phishing:** Targeted phishing from a trusted source familiar to the victim, using information collected via social media
- **Ransomware:** Malicious software designed to block access to a computer system until a sum of money is paid
- **Business Email Compromise (BEC):** Attacker obtains access to a business email account and imitates an executive's identity to defraud the company

> **Key Exam Points: Red Flags (CRITICAL FOR EXAM)** *(pp. 325-347)*
> - Red flags are the single most testable topic in this domain -- expect multiple scenario-based questions
> - A red flag does NOT automatically equal suspicious activity -- it indicates a need for ADDITIONAL SCRUTINY *(p. 325)*
> - The overarching theme: activity that is INCONSISTENT with the customer's known profile, business, or expected behavior
> - Key patterns to recognize across all categories:
>   - **Structuring** -- transactions just below reporting thresholds (US$10,000 for CTR, US$3,000 for recordkeeping)
>   - **Layering** -- rapid movement of funds through multiple accounts, entities, or jurisdictions
>   - **Funnel accounts** -- deposits in one location, quick withdrawals in another
>   - **Third-party involvement** -- unrelated parties making payments, escorting customers, or controlling accounts
>   - **Evasion behavior** -- customer nervousness, threats, reluctance to provide information, asking about reporting requirements
>   - **Geographic anomalies** -- transactions to/from high-risk jurisdictions, secrecy havens, or border areas with no business justification
>   - **Wire stripping** -- employee removes high-risk name from a wire transfer (employee red flag) *(p. 334)*
> - For industry-specific red flags: focus on the UNIQUE characteristics of each industry (e.g., free-look period in insurance, penny stocks in broker-dealer, letter of credit manipulation in trade finance, diamond pipeline in precious metals)
> - Human trafficking red flags focus on PAYROLL anomalies, wage deductions, third-party escorts, and funnel accounts *(pp. 343-345)*
> - Terrorist financing combines BEHAVIORAL indicators (links to known organizations, false IDs, nonprofit abuse) with FINANCIAL indicators (structured deposits, no economic justification, multiple foreign accounts) *(pp. 345-346)*
