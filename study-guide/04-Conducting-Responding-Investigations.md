**Exam Weight: 21% (~25 questions out of 120) | Study Guide Pages 348-391**

## 1. Investigations Initiated by the Financial Organization *(pp. 348-366)*

### 1.1 Sources of Investigations *(pp. 348-356)*

Investigations originate from both **proactive monitoring** (detecting suspicious activity) and **reactive measures** (responding to regulatory findings, referrals, and law enforcement requests). *(p. 348)*

#### Common Investigation Triggers

- **Regulatory recommendations and official findings** *(p. 349)* -- may result in new ongoing monitoring or one-time reviews; must be documented and addressed within regulatory time frames; senior management must be informed of findings and remediation status
- **Transaction monitoring alerts** *(pp. 349-350)* -- automated rules designed to detect suspicious activity; should use a **risk-based approach** considering organization size, products, and customer base; rules must be **regularly reviewed and tuned** (above-the-line and below-the-line testing); the **Wolfsberg Group** recommends the framework be aligned to the business model and embedded in the AML program
- **Referrals from customer-facing employees** *(p. 350)* -- front-line staff report unusual activity (e.g., structuring, currency with unusual odor, inconsistent responses about cash sources); organizations should have internal forms/reporting systems; referral mechanisms should be covered in **employee training programs**
- **Internal hotlines** *(pp. 350-351)* (ethics, compliance, whistleblower hotlines) -- allow anonymous reporting; the organization is **prohibited from retaliating** against the reporter in most jurisdictions; information must be confidentially investigated
- **Negative media information** *(p. 351)* -- organizations should proactively monitor media stories about customers, products, geographic locations, or ML/TF events; must assess whether negative information is financially risk-relevant; may trigger SAR filing and further action
- **Receipt of a governmental subpoena or search warrant** *(pp. 351-352)* -- triggers two independent obligations: (1) legally fulfill the subpoena/warrant requirements, and (2) determine whether the customer activity requires a **SAR filing**
- **Banking regulators** do not need subpoenas or warrants *(p. 352)* -- they have authority to inspect all books and records of a regulated organization

#### Subpoena vs. Search Warrant vs. Freezing Order

| Instrument | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Subpoena** *(p. 352)* | Issued by grand juries; compels production of documents and testimony; organization must confirm legitimacy; comply on a timely and complete basis; **never notify the customer** |
| **Search warrant** *(p. 353)* | Court-granted permission to search premises and seize specific items; based on **probable cause** in an affidavit; is NOT an open-ended demand; does NOT compel testimony |
| **Freezing/restraining order** *(p. 354)* | Court order to freeze accounts or prevent fund withdrawals; based on a sworn affidavit; organization should obtain a copy and make every effort to comply |

#### Responding to a Search Warrant -- Key Steps *(p. 353)*

- Call in-house or outside legal counsel immediately
- Review the warrant to understand its scope
- Obtain a copy of the warrant and request the supporting affidavit
- Remain present while agents make an inventory of seized items
- Get a copy of the agents' seizure inventory
- Document the names and agency affiliations of all agents
- Mark **attorney-client privileged** documents separately; if agents want to seize them, suggest records be given to the court for safekeeping
- Designate **one person** to communicate with the agents

#### National Security Letters (NSLs) *(pp. 354-355)*

- Authorized by USA PATRIOT Act, Electronic Communications Privacy Act, National Security Act, and Fair Credit and Reporting Act
- Can be issued by FBI leadership without judicial approval
- Require third-party businesses to provide data on investigation targets
- The **process is secret** -- recipients are **prohibited from disclosing** receipt of an NSL (gag order)

#### Keeping Records *(p. 355)*

Records to maintain include:
- CDD and EDD information
- SAR filings and internally reported suspicious activity (including cases where no SAR was filed)
- Internal AML controls documentation
- Training records for all relevant staff

> **Key Exam Points: Sources of Investigations**
> - Financial organizations have TWO obligations upon receiving a subpoena: comply with the legal request AND independently determine if a SAR should be filed. *(p. 351)*
> - A subpoena compels documents AND testimony; a search warrant permits search and seizure but does NOT compel testimony. *(pp. 352-353)*
> - NSLs do not require judicial approval and come with a gag order. *(pp. 354-355)*
> - Regulatory agencies do not need subpoenas to examine regulated entities. *(p. 352)*
> - The organization should NEVER notify the customer being investigated. *(p. 352)*
> - If the government asks to keep an account open, get the request in writing with proper letterhead, including the duration; maintain documentation for at least 5 years after the request expires. *(p. 352)*

### 1.2 Conducting the Investigation *(pp. 358-366)*

#### Key Actions in a Financial Investigation *(p. 358)*

- **Review internal transactions** -- value, volume, customer information, and internal documentation
- **Determine if activity is expected** for the customer based on KYC profile
- **Identify and review external information** -- understand the customer, related entities, and relevant media
- **Contact business line employees** responsible for the account relationship
- **Generate a written report** documenting all findings

The investigator's **main objective** is to **track the movement of money** through the financial organization. *(p. 358)*

#### Types of Internal Records Available *(pp. 358-359)*

- Signature cards, account statements, deposit tickets, checks, withdrawal items
- Credit and debit memorandums, loan records
- Cashier's checks, certified checks, traveler's checks, money orders
- Wire transfer records, safe deposit box records, credit card records
- Login activity logs, IP addresses, geographical location data (for online organizations)

**Record retention**: Many jurisdictions (EU, US, Canada) require keeping customer account records for **five years**. *(p. 359)*

#### Using the Internet for Investigations (OSINT) *(pp. 360-362)*

**Open-source intelligence (OSINT)** is information obtained from publicly available data, used in financial crime investigations and KYC processes. *(p. 365)*

- **Reliable sources**: FATF, Wolfsberg, OECD, supervisory authority websites, corporate registrars, electoral rolls, court records, administrative tribunals *(pp. 360-361)*
- **Social media** (Facebook, LinkedIn) -- can verify some information but blogs/comments may not be reliable *(p. 360)*
- **Verify negative news** from multiple independent sources; consider journalist bias and press freedom in the relevant country *(p. 361)*
- **Commercial databases/public record aggregators** -- cross-reference large numbers of records including PII not available on the open internet *(p. 362)*

#### OSINT Investigation Best Practices *(p. 362)*

- Prepare a search plan before starting, focusing on topics and types of information needed
- Use **metasearch engines** first, then specific search engines
- Use **Boolean operators** (AND, OR) to refine searches
- Use **multiple search engines** -- no single engine covers the entire web
- Use local search engines when searching in a foreign country
- Use **online translation tools** for foreign-language content
- Use **online map applications** to verify physical locations
- Retain relevant records via screen capture or downloads (websites change)
- Always complement internet research with **internal documents and records**

> **Key Exam Points: Conducting Investigations**
> - The investigator's primary objective is to TRACK THE MOVEMENT OF MONEY. *(p. 358)*
> - Investigations should use BOTH internal records AND external/open-source information. *(p. 360)*
> - OSINT is a key tool: uses search engines, keyword searches, social media, public registries, and news sites. *(p. 365)*
> - Always verify negative news from multiple independent sources. *(p. 361)*
> - Financial organizations should have documented policies and procedures identifying procedural steps, required information, and recommended next steps for investigations. *(p. 359)*

### 1.3 SAR Decision-Making Process *(pp. 367-369)*

#### When to File *(p. 367)*

- The decision involves weighing **aggravating factors** vs. **mitigating factors** from the investigation
- Organizations should have documented procedures listing factors to consider
- Properly trained personnel should have a **clear escalation procedure** to a compliance officer, manager, or authorized staff member
- The **final decision must be documented** with supporting reasoning
- The reason **NOT** to file is as important as the reason to file

#### What to Include in a SAR *(pp. 367-368)*

- Details of the suspicious activity and related demographics
- The reasons the organization finds the activity suspicious
- Any known **typologies** identified during the review
- Context about what is expected activity for the organization's customer base, products, and geographic area (the recipient of the SAR does not have this knowledge)

#### SAR Filing Timeline and Notifications *(p. 368)*

- Many jurisdictions require SARs to be filed within a **specific number of days** following discovery
- If filing, notify investigators/prosecutors **as soon as possible**
- Failure to file timely reports is **frequently cited in regulatory actions** against financial organizations
- **Senior management and/or the board** should receive information about SAR filings (number of reports, dollar amounts, significant trends)
- Under **BSA record-retention requirements**, maintain SAR records and supporting documentation for **five years** following the date filed

#### Quality Assurance and Quality Control (QA/QC) *(p. 368)*

- Ensures SAR filings are **internally consistent** and that the right decisions are being made
- High-priority issues should be identified and **escalated to leadership**
- Organizations should document QA/QC reviewer requirements and qualifications
- Regularly review outcomes to assess staff quality, training needs, and program health

#### SAR Filing Oversight and Escalation *(p. 369)*

- Robust policies should include specific actions for escalation -- e.g., when a customer-facing employee or someone in the AML/CFT chain of command is **complicit or willfully blind** to suspicious activities

> **Key Exam Points: SAR Decision-Making**
> - The SAR decision is based on AGGRAVATING vs. MITIGATING factors plus knowledge of expected activity. *(p. 367)*
> - Both the decision TO file and NOT to file must be documented with reasoning. *(p. 367)*
> - SARs must be filed within jurisdiction-specific timeframes -- untimely filing is a common regulatory violation. *(p. 368)*
> - SAR records must be maintained for 5 years (BSA requirement). *(p. 368)*
> - QA/QC processes ensure consistency and quality in SAR filings. *(p. 368)*
> - Senior management/board must be kept informed of SAR metrics and significant trends. *(p. 368)*
> - On a scenario question: if activity cannot be explained by the customer's profile, a SAR should likely be filed.

### 1.4 Failing to Report SARs -- Consequences *(pp. 369-372)*

#### Key Principles *(pp. 371)*

- SARs are the **"lifeblood"** of financial investigations for law enforcement
- Designated persons (**MLRO**, **BSA officer**) "report" or "sign" as the reporter to the FIU
- Reporting suspicious activity is a **collective responsibility**, but reporting to the FIU is the responsibility of the **designated officer**
- Failure to disclose information on suspected ML activity can result in a **five-year prison sentence** (UK, under Section 330 of the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002) *(p. 369)*
- Regulators have taken action against numerous organizations for failure to report

#### Case Examples

- **Dominic Thorncroft (UK, 2021)** *(pp. 369-370)*: MLRO for an MSB who failed to file SARs despite knowing his business was being used to transfer money to fraudsters; received 18-month suspended sentence and 250 hours of unpaid work
- **Deutsche Bank (2020)** *(pp. 371-372)*: Fined EUR 13.5 million for failing to submit SARs in a timely fashion related to over 1 million suspicious money transfers involving Danske Bank Estonia; between 2010-2015, failed to send timely alerts on 627 occasions

> **Key Exam Points: Failing to Report SARs**
> - Designated persons (MLROs, BSA officers) can be PERSONALLY held accountable for failure to file SARs. *(p. 369)*
> - In the UK, failure to disclose suspected ML can result in up to 5 years in prison. *(p. 369)*
> - Organizations in regulated sectors must exercise a GREATER level of diligence. *(p. 371)*
> - Even if the designated person did not commit the fraud themselves, failing to report it carries severe consequences. *(p. 370)*

### 1.5 Closing the Account *(p. 372)*

Factors to consider when deciding whether to close an account:

- **Legal basis** for closing the account
- Organization's **policies and procedures** (some include automatic closure recommendation after a specified number of SAR filings)
- **Seriousness of the underlying conduct** -- does it rise to the level at which accounts are typically closed?
- **Reputational risk** to the organization from maintaining the account
- **Law enforcement correspondence** -- requests to either cancel or maintain the account

> **Key Exam Points: Closing Accounts**
> - The decision to close is the organization's INDEPENDENT decision. *(p. 372)*
> - Law enforcement may request keeping an account open for investigative purposes -- get this in writing.
> - Account closure is NOT automatic upon filing a SAR; it depends on multiple factors including organizational policies and the severity of the conduct. *(p. 372)*

---

### 1.6 Communicating with Law Enforcement on SARs *(p. 373)*

- SARs can get **lost in the large volume** of reports filed with the FIU
- After filing, the compliance officer/designee can **proactively contact** a specific law enforcement division to notify them
- Law enforcement can also **contact the filing organization** to seek underlying investigation information
- Each organization should develop its own **policies and procedures** for communicating with law enforcement regarding SARs

## 2. Investigations Initiated by Law Enforcement *(pp. 374-383)*

### 2.1 Law Enforcement Investigation Steps *(pp. 374-375)*

- **Follow the money** -- trace the flow of funds from origin to destination *(p. 374)*
- **Leverage financial knowledge** in financial organizations through information sharing and transactional reviews; supporting CDD/SAR documentation can be used as **evidence** (actual SARs may not be admissible in many jurisdictions) *(p. 374)*
- **Identify the unlawful activity** -- prosecutors must establish both the flow of money AND the existence of a **predicate offense** (e.g., bribery, narcotics trafficking, racketeering) *(p. 374)*
- **Review databases** -- FIU databases, commercial databases, Social Security/tax information *(p. 374)*
- **Review public records** -- court records, corporate filings, credit reports *(p. 375)*
- **Review licensing and registration files** -- motor vehicle departments, registration databases *(p. 375)*
- **Analyze financial transactions and account activity** -- compare against typical/expected activity for similarly situated individuals *(p. 375)*
- **Review related SARs** *(p. 375)*
- In cross-border cases, **seek international assistance** *(p. 375)*

### 2.2 Decision to Prosecute *(pp. 375-376)*

Factors prosecutors consider when deciding whether to bring charges:

- Organization has a **criminal history**
- Organization has **cooperated** with the investigation
- Organization **discovered and self-reported** the issues
- Organization has a **comprehensive and effective AML/CFT program**
- Organization has taken **timely and effective remedial action**
- **Civil remedies** are available as alternative punishment
- **Deterrence** of others would be served by prosecution
- Advice from **regulatory agencies and/or the FIU**

The decision often comes down to what prosecutors believe was the **intent** of the organization. *(p. 376)*

### 2.3 Responding to a Law Enforcement Investigation *(p. 376)*

When confronted with an investigation:

- **Respond quickly and completely** to all requests -- never ignore or delay
- If a request is overly broad or intrusive, the organization can seek to **narrow or contest** it in court
- **Inform senior management** and designate an individual to handle all law enforcement requests
- Monitor the investigation and keep **senior management and the board** informed
- **Do NOT share** investigation information with employees, officers, or directors who might be implicated
- Retain **qualified legal counsel**

### 2.4 Monitoring the Investigation *(pp. 377-378)*

- Ensure all **grand jury subpoenas and government requests** are reviewed by senior management and/or legal counsel
- Maintain **centralized control** over all requests and responses
- Maintain a **complete record** of all information provided
- Ensure relevant documents are **not altered, lost, or destroyed** -- advise all employees of this *(p. 377)*
- Address the organization's **document destruction policy** to ensure no documents are destroyed during the investigation
- **Key point**: Destroying documents after learning of an investigation -- even before receiving a subpoena -- is a **serious concern** *(p. 377)*
- Create a **privilege log** and separate privileged documents from general records *(p. 378)*
- Set up a system to organize, maintain, secure, copy, number, and index documents for production

### 2.5 Cooperating with Law Enforcement *(p. 378)*

- Cooperation can be the **most effective way to terminate an investigation** before it damages the organization
- Forms of cooperation: making employees available for interviews, producing documents without a subpoena, **voluntary disclosure** by sharing counsel's reports
- Maintain a **positive working relationship** with investigators and prosecutors
- Try to learn how investigators view the facts -- if they are incorrect, the organization can correct them

### 2.6 Obtaining Counsel *(pp. 379-380)*

#### Retaining Counsel *(p. 379)*

- Recommended for large, important, or serious investigations
- **In-house counsel** -- less expensive, better knowledge of organization; appropriate for most situations
- **Outside counsel** -- more appropriate if conduct could lead to **criminal investigation or indictment**
- Ensure counsel is experienced in financial organization defense matters
- Senior management should be aware of and support counsel's involvement

#### Attorney-Client Privilege *(p. 379)*

- Attorneys represent the **entity**, not its individual employees
- Work product and communications may be protected under **attorney-client privilege**
- When interests of the entity and employees **diverge or conflict**, separate counsel may be required

#### Disseminating Counsel's Written Report *(p. 380)*

- Every page should state it is **confidential and subject to attorney-client and work-product privilege**
- Copies should be numbered with a tracked distribution list
- Recipients should not make notes on their copies
- All copies maintained in a file **separate from regular organization files**
- Copies should be returned after a specific time period

### 2.7 Employee Notices and Interviews *(pp. 380-381)*

- Employees should be **informed of the investigation** and instructed NOT to directly produce corporate documents to law enforcement *(p. 380)*
- All requests for documents and interviews should go through **senior management or counsel**
- Interview all knowledgeable employees **as soon as possible** while memories are fresh *(p. 381)*
- **Prepare employees** before law enforcement interviews and **debrief them** afterward
- All interview requests should be routed through a **single person or centralized location**
- Interviews should be **nonconfrontational** -- start with background/open-ended questions; delay contentious questions to the end *(p. 381)*

### 2.8 Media Relations *(p. 382)*

- Public perception is vital to maintaining **public trust**
- If the facts are not on the organization's side, **"no comment"** may be the best response
- **Misleading or false statements** can worsen the situation and invite additional regulatory/law enforcement scrutiny (especially for publicly traded companies)
- Cooperation with regulators can lead to **reduced fines** (e.g., Credit Suisse received a 30% penalty discount for cooperating with UK FCA, US DOJ, and Swiss authorities) *(pp. 382-383)*

#### Case Example: Credit Suisse *(pp. 382-383)*

In October 2021, the UK FCA fined Credit Suisse over GBP 147 million for serious anti-financial crime (AFC) due diligence failures related to loans to the Republic of Mozambique. Credit Suisse agreed to pay US$475 million in fines and penalties as part of a coordinated international resolution with criminal and civil authorities in the US, Switzerland, and the UK. The FCA stated that the bank was awarded a **30% discount in the overall penalty due to their cooperation**. Without this cooperation, a significantly larger penalty would have been imposed. *(p. 383)*

> **Key Exam Points: Law Enforcement Investigations**
> - The decision to prosecute heavily weighs the organization's INTENT, COOPERATION, and quality of its AML program. *(p. 376)*
> - NEVER ignore or delay responding to law enforcement -- but you CAN seek to narrow overly broad requests. *(p. 376)*
> - Do NOT share investigation details with potentially implicated employees. *(p. 376)*
> - Document destruction after learning of an investigation is a SERIOUS issue -- even before a subpoena is received. *(p. 377)*
> - Cooperation can significantly reduce penalties (Credit Suisse received 30% discount). *(p. 383)*
> - Attorneys represent the ENTITY, not individual employees; separate counsel may be needed if interests conflict. *(p. 379)*
> - SAR supporting documentation CAN be used as evidence; the SAR itself may NOT be admissible. *(p. 374)*

## 3. AML/CFT Cooperation between Countries *(pp. 384-391)*

### 3.1 FATF Recommendations 36-40 on International Cooperation *(p. 384)*

- Practices restricting international cooperation between supervisory authorities or FIUs are **serious obstacles** to combating money laundering
- **Recommendations 36-40** address the international aspects of ML/TF investigations:
  - **Mutual legal assistance treaties** (MLATs)
  - **Extradition**
  - **Confiscation of assets**
  - **Mechanisms to exchange information** internationally

### 3.2 SHERLOC (Sharing Electronic Resources and Laws on Crime) *(pp. 384-385)*

- A **knowledge management portal** of ML information for national and international AML agencies
- Developed and administered by the **United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime** (UNODC)

**Six main features:**
1. **Legislation Database** -- compendium of national AML laws and regulations *(p. 384)*
2. **Reference data** -- research, analysis, bibliography, treaties *(p. 385)*
3. **Country page** -- full text of AML legislation and links to national FIUs *(p. 385)*
4. **Case Law Database** -- summaries of cases about 15 different crime types *(p. 385)*
5. **International norms and standards** -- UN conventions, model laws *(p. 385)*
6. **Directory of Competent National Authorities (CAN Directory)** -- facilitates international cooperation; restricted to competent national authorities *(p. 385)*

### 3.3 Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs) *(pp. 385-386)*

- Provide the **legal basis** for transmitting evidence between countries that can be used for prosecution and judicial proceedings
- MLATs are **bilateral** (between two countries at a time)
- Cover: taking of evidence, provision of information and documents, search and seizure, locating persons and objects, service of documents, measures to locate/restrain/forfeit proceeds of crime

#### Typical MLAT Process *(pp. 385-386)*

1. The **central authority** of the requesting country sends a **commission rogatoire** (letters rogatory/letter of request) to the central authority of the other country, including the information sought, nature of request, criminal charges, and legal provision
2. The receiving central authority sends it to a **local financial investigator** to determine information availability
3. An investigator from the requesting country **visits** and accompanies the local investigator
4. The investigator asks the central authority for **permission to remove evidence**
5. The central authority sends the evidence to the requesting central authority

### 3.4 Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs) *(pp. 386-389)*

#### Definition and Core Functions *(pp. 386-387)*

**FIUs** are mandatory national agencies that handle financial intelligence. They have **three basic functions**:

1. **Receive** -- reports of suspicious transactions from financial organizations
2. **Analyze** -- the reported information
3. **Disseminate** -- resulting intelligence to local law enforcement and foreign FIUs

**FATF Recommendation 29**: Countries should establish an FIU as a **national center** for the receipt and analysis of (a) suspicious transaction reports and (b) other information relevant to money laundering, predicate offenses, and terrorist financing. *(pp. 387-388)*

#### FIU Powers (FATF Recommendations 30-31) *(p. 388)*

- Responsibility for ML/TF investigations within national AML/CFT policy framework
- Develop proactive parallel financial investigations when pursuing ML and predicate offenses
- Identify, trace, and initiate **freeze and seizure** actions on property suspected as proceeds of crime
- Access all necessary documents using compulsory measures (production of records, search of persons/premises, witness statements, seizure of evidence)
- Use a wide range of **investigative techniques** (undercover operations, intercepting communications, accessing computer systems, controlled delivery)

#### Key FIUs to Know *(pp. 388-389)*

| FIU | Country | Key Details |
|---|---|---|
| **FinCEN** | United States | Established 1990; mission to safeguard financial system from illicit use; collects, analyzes, and disseminates financial intelligence *(p. 389)* |
| **AUSTRAC** | Australia | Founded 1989; Australia's primary source for financial intelligence; fights serious and organized crime and terrorism financing *(p. 388)* |
| **NCA (National Crime Agency)** | United Kingdom | Operational since 2013; leads UK law enforcement's fight against serious and organized crime; the UK FIU operates within the NCA *(p. 388)* |

#### FIU Information Sharing *(p. 389)*

- FIUs share information informally on the basis of **memoranda of understanding (MOUs)** -- unlike MLATs, MOUs are used for obtaining **intelligence** (not evidence directly), but intelligence can lead to evidence
- The **Egmont Group** has established a model for MOUs

### 3.5 Egmont Group *(pp. 389-391)*

- An international association of FIUs that share the core functions of **receiving, analyzing, and disseminating** financial information to combat ML/TF
- In 2001, adopted **"Principles of Information Exchange between Financial Intelligence Units"** incorporated into its Statement of Purpose *(p. 389)*

#### Key Egmont Principles *(pp. 389-390)*

- Free exchange of information at the FIU level based on **reciprocity**, including spontaneous exchange
- Differences in definitions of offenses should **NOT be an obstacle** to information exchange
- Exchange should be as **informal and rapid** as possible with no excessive formal prerequisites
- FIUs should have authority to sign **MOUs independently** (without undue delay)
- **Direct communication** between FIUs without intermediaries
- Consent to disseminate information for law enforcement/judicial purposes should be granted **promptly and to the greatest extent possible**
- Information exchange should use the **Egmont Secure Web (ESW)** *(p. 390)*

#### FIU.net *(pp. 386-387)*

- A decentralized network for European FIUs to exchange information
- Pilot started in **2004** with FIUs of Luxembourg, UK, Italy, France, and the Netherlands (Dutch FIU) *(p. 386)*
- Features include **Ma3tch** -- a tool to match and detect common subjects without exposing irrelevant information *(p. 387)*

#### Egmont Group Revised Charter Goals *(p. 391)*

- Unite efforts to improve **exchange of information** upon request and spontaneously
- Exchange information on respective experiences to promote effective FIU development
- Support members by promoting **operational independence**, offering training and technical assistance, developing collaboration, and maintaining the **Egmont Secure Web**

> **Key Exam Points: International Cooperation**
> - The THREE basic functions of an FIU: **Receive, Analyze, Disseminate** (memorize this). *(p. 387)*
> - FATF Recommendations 36-40 cover international cooperation (MLATs, extradition, asset confiscation, information exchange). *(p. 384)*
> - MLATs are BILATERAL treaties providing a LEGAL BASIS for transmitting evidence between countries. *(p. 385)*
> - MOUs are used by FIUs for sharing INTELLIGENCE (not evidence directly); the Egmont Group provides the MOU model. *(p. 389)*
> - The Egmont Group promotes information exchange on the basis of RECIPROCITY with no excessive formal prerequisites. *(p. 389)*
> - The Egmont Secure Web (ESW) is the secure channel for FIU-to-FIU information exchange. *(p. 390)*
> - SHERLOC is a UN-administered knowledge management portal with 6 features including a legislation database and case law database. *(pp. 384-385)*
> - Know the key FIUs: FinCEN (US, est. 1990), AUSTRAC (Australia, est. 1989), NCA (UK, operational 2013). *(pp. 388-389)*
> - FIU.net is a European FIU network; Ma3tch is its tool for matching common subjects. *(pp. 386-387)*
