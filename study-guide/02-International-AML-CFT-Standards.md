## 1. Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

### 1.1 Overview and History

- Founded in **1989** by the **G-7** nations at their Paris economic summit; France served as first chair
- Originally called the G-7 Financial Action Task Force
- Intergovernmental body based at the **OECD in Paris**
- Serves as the **vanguard** for promulgating AML guidance worldwide
- **Cannot impose fines or penalties** on members -- uses "graduated approach aimed at enhancing **peer pressure**"
- Operated under five-year mandates until 2019, when it adopted an **open-ended mandate** on its 30th anniversary
- Three main activities: **(1)** standard setting, **(2)** ensuring compliance, **(3)** identifying ML/TF threats

### 1.2 FATF Objectives

FATF's stated objectives: **set standards and promote effective implementation** of legal, regulatory, and operational measures for combating ML, TF, and other related threats.

FATF fulfills objectives by:
- **Spreading the AML message worldwide** -- expanding membership, developing regional bodies, cooperating with international organizations
- **Monitoring implementation** of FATF Recommendations among members
- **Reviewing ML trends and countermeasures**

### 1.3 Mutual Evaluations

#### Technical Compliance Assessment
- Evaluates specific requirements of the FATF Recommendations
- Focus: fundamental **building blocks** of an AML/CFT system
- **Five ratings:** Compliant, Largely Compliant, Partially Compliant, Noncompliant, Not Applicable

#### Effectiveness Assessment
- Assesses adequacy of a member's **implementation** of the Recommendations
- Based on **11 Immediate Outcomes** (e.g., ML risks are known, international cooperation works, FIs apply preventive measures)
- Feeds into **3 Intermediate Outcomes:**
  1. Policy, cooperation, and coordination to mitigate ML/TF
  2. Prevention of proceeds of crime entering the financial system
  3. Detection and disruption of ML/TF threats
- **Effectiveness ratings:** High, Substantial, Moderate, Low
- Under the **fourth round**, follow-up assessments conducted after **5 years**

> **Key Exam Points -- FATF Overview:**
> - FATF was created in 1989 by the G-7 in Paris
> - FATF has no enforcement power -- relies on peer pressure
> - Mutual evaluations have two components: technical compliance (5 ratings) and effectiveness (4 ratings, 11 Immediate Outcomes)
> - Turkey was the first country subjected to FATF peer pressure (1996)
> - FATF adopted an open-ended mandate in 2019

### 1.4 FATF 40 Recommendations

- First issued in **1990**, revised in **2003** and **2012**
- After 9/11 (2001), FATF issued **IX Special Recommendations** on terrorist financing, later **merged into the 40** in 2012
- Recognized by the **IMF and World Bank** as the international standard for combating ML/TF
- Set **minimum standards** -- countries implement according to their own legal frameworks

#### Key 2003 Revision Changes:
- Expanded coverage to include **terrorist financing**
- Widened covered businesses: real estate agents, precious metals dealers, accountants, lawyers, trust services
- Specified CDD and EDD procedures
- Clearer definition of ML **predicate offenses**
- Prohibition of **shell banks**
- Improved transparency of legal persons/arrangements
- Stronger international cooperation safeguards

#### Key 2012 Revision Changes:
- **Risk-based approach** made the first recommendation (Rec. 1)
- Incorporated the IX Special Recommendations on TF
- Added targeted financial sanctions for **WMD proliferation**
- Expanded PEP scope to include **domestic PEPs** and international organization officials
- Required risk assessment of **new products** before launch
- **Travel rule**: accurate originator, intermediary, beneficiary info for wire transfers
- **Group-wide AML/CFT programs** required for financial groups
- **Tax crimes** included as designated category of ML offenses

#### FATF 40 Recommendations Reference Table

| Group | Topic | Rec. Numbers |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| **I** | AML/CFT Policies and Coordination (risk assessment, risk-based approach, national cooperation) | 1-2 |
| **II** | Money Laundering and Confiscation (ML offenses, confiscation and provisional measures) | 3-4 |
| **III** | Terrorist Financing and Financing of Proliferation (TF offenses, targeted financial sanctions for terrorism and proliferation, NPOs) | 5-8 |
| **IV** | Financial and Nonfinancial Institution Preventive Measures (secrecy laws, CDD, recordkeeping, additional measures for specific customers, reliance/controls/groups, reporting, DNFBPs) | 9-23 |
| **V** | Transparency and Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons and Arrangements | 24-25 |
| **VI** | Powers and Responsibilities of Competent Authorities (regulation, supervision, law enforcement, sanctions) | 26-35 |
| **VII** | International Cooperation (instruments, mutual legal assistance, extradition, other cooperation) | 36-40 |

#### Highlights of the 40 Recommendations

- **Risk-based approach**: Countries and FIs should identify, assess, and understand their ML/TF risks, then take appropriate measures
- **Designated categories of offenses**: Specified predicate crimes for ML; countries must allow confiscation of proceeds
- **TF and proliferation financing**: Criminalize TF even if no terrorist act is attributed; impose sanction regimes to freeze assets per UNSC designations
- **Knowledge and criminal liability**: Knowledge can be inferred from **objective factual circumstances** ("willful blindness"); liability should apply to legal persons too
- **CDD measures**: Required when establishing business relations, carrying out occasional transactions above **US$15,000/EUR15,000**, for wire transfers per Rec. 16, suspicion of ML/TF, or doubts about prior CID info
- **CDD components:**
  - Identify and verify customer identity
  - Identify **beneficial owner**
  - Understand purpose/nature of business relationship
  - Conduct **ongoing due diligence**
  - Maintain records
  - May rely on third parties (but relying institution remains liable)
  - Group-wide AML/CFT program
- **Additional CDD for specific customers:** PEPs (senior management approval, source of wealth/funds), cross-border correspondent banking, MVTS, new products/technologies
- **Wire transfers (Travel Rule - Rec. 16):** Obtain/send accurate originator, intermediary, and beneficiary info; applies to cross-border and domestic transfers; de minimis threshold of **USD/EUR 1,000** for cross-border
- **SAR reporting:** Report to FIU when suspecting funds are proceeds of crime or related to TF; protected from liability; confidentiality of SARs is critical
- **Derisking:** Terminating relationships to avoid risk rather than manage it; FATF only requires termination on a case-by-case basis when ML/TF risks cannot be mitigated
- **DNFBPs** (Designated Nonfinancial Businesses and Professions): Casinos, real estate agents, dealers in precious metals/stones, lawyers/notaries/accountants, trust and company service providers
- **Thresholds:** Occasional customers US$15,000; casinos US$3,000; precious metals US$15,000
- **Transparency of legal persons/arrangements:** Beneficial ownership info must be available to competent authorities
- **International cooperation:** Mutual legal assistance, extradition, ratify UN conventions

> **Key Exam Points -- FATF 40 Recommendations:**
> - Risk-based approach is Recommendation 1 (since 2012)
> - IX Special Recommendations on TF were merged into the 40 in 2012
> - Knowledge of ML can be inferred from objective factual circumstances
> - CDD threshold for occasional transactions: US$15,000/EUR15,000
> - Wire transfer de minimis threshold: USD/EUR 1,000
> - DNFBPs include casinos, real estate agents, precious metals dealers, lawyers, accountants, TCSPs
> - FATF Recommendations are recognized by IMF and World Bank as the international standard

### 1.5 FATF Members and Observers

- Originally **16 member jurisdictions**; now represents most major financial centers
- **Membership criteria** (quantitative): GDP, size of banking/insurance/securities sectors, population
- **Membership criteria** (qualitative): Impact on global financial system, active FSRB participation, level of AML/CFT risks
- **Additional considerations**: Adherence to financial sector standards, participation in international organizations, geographic balance
- **Membership process** (2 steps):
  1. Engage with country, grant observership (written political commitment, agree to mutual evaluation)
  2. Carry out mutual evaluation, agree on action plan, grant membership

### 1.6 Noncooperative Countries and Territories (NCCTs)

- FATF has a practice of **"naming and shaming"** countries with inadequate AML controls
- **NCCT process** began in 2000; used **25 criteria** in four broad areas:
  1. Loopholes in financial regulations
  2. Obstacles raised by other regulatory requirements
  3. Obstacles to international cooperation
  4. Inadequate resources for preventing/detecting ML
- NCCT process ultimately involved **24 jurisdictions** (up to 19 at one time)
- Now replaced by two public documents issued **three times a year:**

#### FATF's Two Public Lists

| List | Also Known As | Description |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| **High-Risk Jurisdictions Subject to a Call for Action** | **"Blacklist"** | Strategic deficiencies so serious that FATF calls for EDD and, in worst cases, countermeasures |
| **Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring** | **"Greylist"** | Countries actively engaging with FATF to address strategic deficiencies; committed to resolving within agreed timeframe |

- **ICRG** (International Cooperation Review Group) oversees the process of identifying jurisdictions with strategic deficiencies
- Review triggered when: jurisdiction refuses FSRB participation, does not publish mutual evaluation results, or achieves poor results (20+ NC/PC ratings, or low effectiveness for 6+ of 11 outcomes)
- Jurisdictions get a **one-year observation period** before possible public identification

> **Key Exam Points -- NCCTs and Lists:**
> - NCCT process used 25 criteria in 4 areas
> - Now replaced by two documents: "Blacklist" (Call for Action) and "Greylist" (Increased Monitoring)
> - Published three times a year
> - ICRG oversees the review process
> - Turkey was the first country added to the Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring list

## 2. Basel Committee on Banking Supervision

### 2.1 Overview

- Established in **1974** by **central bank governors of the G-10 countries**
- **Primary global standard-setter** for prudential regulation of banks
- Forum for cooperation on banking supervisory matters
- Best known for **Basel I, Basel II, Basel III** (capital adequacy)
- Secretariat located at the **Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in Basel, Switzerland**

### 2.2 History of AML Guidance

- **1988**: Issued *Statement of Principles: Prevention of Criminal Use of the Banking System for the Purpose of Money Laundering* -- first AML commitment; covered customer identification, compliance with laws, ethical standards, cooperation with law enforcement, staff training, recordkeeping
- **1997**: *Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision* -- basic reference for authorities worldwide; urged adoption of FATF 40 Recommendations; **BCP 29** specifically addresses abuse of financial services
- **2001**: *Customer Due Diligence for Banks* -- account opening and CID guidelines
- **2003**: Account opening and customer identification guidelines
- **2004**: *Consolidated KYC Risk Management* -- global approach to KYC across parent bank and all branches/subsidiaries

### 2.3 Four Key Elements of a KYC Program (Basel)

1. **Customer identification**
2. **Risk management**
3. **Customer acceptance policy**
4. **Ongoing monitoring**

#### Key KYC Guidance Points:
- Banks must monitor account activity for transactions that do not conform to normal/expected patterns
- **Numbered accounts** should not be prohibited but must be subject to same KYC procedures
- Private banking accounts must **never** escape KYC policies
- Standard ID procedures for **non-face-to-face** customers; never open accounts for those insistent on anonymity
- **High-risk customer ID issues**: trusts/nominee/fiduciary accounts, corporate vehicles with bearer shares, introduced businesses, accounts opened by professional intermediaries, PEPs, correspondent banking
- Bank regulators should ensure staff follow KYC procedures and take action against officers who fail

### 2.4 Sound Management of ML/TF Risks (2014 Guidelines, Updated 2020)

Superseded previous publications on CDD and KYC risk management. Key controls:

- **Risk analysis and governance**: Identify and analyze ML risks; document assessment; make available to supervisors
- **Governance/tone from the top**: Board approves policies; appoint qualified **chief AML officer** with sufficient authority and resources; must have independence from business lines
- **Three lines of defense:**
  1. **Line of business** (first): Creates/implements policies, screens employees, delivers training
  2. **AML compliance and internal control functions** (second): Ongoing monitoring, sample testing, exception reports; AML officer is contact point for all AML issues
  3. **Audit function** (third): Reports to board/audit committee; independently evaluates risk management and controls; periodic assessments; enterprise-wide audit
- **CDD and acceptance**: Develop customer acceptance policy; basic DD for all, increased DD as risk increases; some customers eligible for simplified DD
- **Minimum individual info**: Legal name, residential address, unique ID number, date and place of birth
- **Four steps for verifying customers**: (1) Identification of customer and beneficial owners, (2) Assessment of risk profile, (3) Verification of identity, (4) Further risk-based verification (source of wealth/funds)
- **When CDD cannot be completed**: Do not open account, or close existing account; consider reporting as suspicious
- **Transaction monitoring**: Adequate automated systems; cover all accounts; trend analysis; enterprise-level view
- **Management of information**: Retain records at least **5 years** after closing account; document SAR decisions
- **Reporting and asset freezing**: Identify unusual activity, eliminate false positives, report in timely/confidential manner; screen against sanctions lists
- **2020 update**: Added guidance on cooperation between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors

> **Key Exam Points -- Basel Committee:**
> - Established 1974 by G-10 central bank governors; located in Basel, Switzerland
> - 1988 Statement of Principles was first AML commitment
> - Four elements of KYC: customer identification, risk management, customer acceptance policy, ongoing monitoring
> - Three lines of defense: (1) line of business, (2) compliance/internal control, (3) audit
> - Minimum individual CDD info: name, address, ID number, date/place of birth
> - Records retained at least 5 years after account closing
> - Numbered accounts are permitted but must follow same KYC procedures

## 3. European Union Directives on Money Laundering

- EU Directives are issued by the European Parliament and implemented by member states as domestic legislation
- **EU law prevails over national law** in the case of directives
- Directives apply only to **EU member states** (not other countries)
- Have far more weight than voluntary standards (e.g., FATF, Basel)

### 3.1 Progression of EU AML Directives

| Directive | Year | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------|---------------------------|
| **First** (91/308/EEC) | 1991 | Required legislation to prevent financial systems from being used for ML; predicate offenses confined to **drug trafficking** only (per 1988 Vienna Convention); encouraged extending to other crimes |
| **Second** (2001/97/EEC) | 2001 | Extended predicates beyond drugs to **all serious crimes**; added currency exchanges and money remittance offices; widened covered professions (auditors, accountants, tax advisors, real estate agents, notaries, lawyers); knowledge inferable from objective factual circumstances |
| **Third** (2005/60/EC) | 2005 | Based on FATF revised 40 Recs; defined ML and **TF as separate crimes**; introduced **risk-based approach** to CDD; required **beneficial owner ID&V** (>25% control); employee whistleblower protection; SAR statistics; added trust/company service providers, cash dealers >EUR15,000; defined PEPs |
| **Fourth** (EU 2015/849) | 2015 | **Repealed Third Directive**; lowered cash threshold to **EUR10,000** (from EUR15,000); expanded to all **gambling services** (not just casinos); CDD for transfers >**EUR1,000**; tax crimes as predicate; EDD for **all PEPs** (domestic and third-country) for at least **12 months**; beneficial ownership **central registers**; penalties up to EUR5 million or 10% annual turnover; member states conduct national AML/CFT risk assessments |
| **Fifth** (EU 2018/843) | 2018 | **Publicly accessible** beneficial ownership registers (excluding trusts); strengthened regulator cooperation/info sharing; EDD for high-risk third countries; lowered **prepaid card** anonymous threshold to **EUR150** in-store / **EUR50** online; published PEP lists; extended scope to **art traders** and **custodian wallet providers**; **crypto-to-fiat exchanges** must have AML controls and be licensed/registered |
| **Sixth** (6AMLD) | 2024 | Establishes **EU AML Authority (AMLA)** for central supervision; only EU parliament approval needed (not member state); registries for **beneficial owners, bank accounts, safe deposit boxes, real estate**; enhanced **FIU** provisions (independence, powers to suspend accounts); AML **supervisory colleges**; complemented by Criminal Law Directive (22 ML offenses, covers cybercrime and environmental crime); criminal liability for organizations |

> **Key Exam Points -- EU Directives:**
> - First Directive (1991): Drug trafficking only as predicate
> - Second (2001): Extended to all serious crimes, added more professions
> - Third (2005): TF as separate crime, risk-based CDD, beneficial owner = >25% control
> - Fourth (2015): Repealed Third; EDD for all PEPs (domestic + foreign) for 12+ months; cash threshold lowered to EUR10,000; central BO registers
> - Fifth (2018): Public BO registers; crypto exchanges covered; prepaid card thresholds lowered
> - Sixth (2024): Creates AMLA; registries for BO, bank accounts, real estate; 22 ML offense categories
> - EU directives have force of law (unlike FATF Recommendations)

## 4. FATF-Style Regional Bodies (FSRBs)

- **Nine FSRBs**, all considered FATF **associate members**
- Similar form and functions to FATF; provide input on standards and typologies
- FATF remains the **only standard-setting body**
- Key principles: Autonomy, reciprocity, common interest, shared objectives

| FSRB | Region | Established | Headquarters | Key Facts |
|------|--------|------------|--------------|-----------|
| **APG** | Asia/Pacific | 1997 | Sydney, Australia | Voluntary and cooperative; not derived from treaty; does not require existing AML/CFT laws to participate |
| **CFATF** | Caribbean Basin, Central/South America | 1990 (Aruba) / 1992 (Jamaica) | Port of Spain, Trinidad and Tobago | 27 member states; Kingston Ministerial Declaration; 19 Aruba Recommendations complement FATF 40 |
| **MONEYVAL** | Council of Europe members (not in FATF) | 1997 | Strasbourg, France | FATF associate member since 2006; independent monitoring mechanism within Council of Europe since 2011 |
| **GAFILAT** | Latin America | 2000 | Buenos Aires, Argentina | Formerly GAFISUD; created in Cartagena, Colombia; uses training and mutual evaluations as main tools |
| **GIABA** | West Africa | 1999 | Dakar, Senegal | Body of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS); revised mandate in 2006 to include TF |
| **MENAFATF** | Middle East and North Africa | 2004 | Manama, Bahrain | Voluntary; established at inaugural meeting in Bahrain; 14 original member countries |
| **EAG** | Eurasia | 2004 | Moscow, Russia | Created for Eurasian countries not in other FSRBs; members include Belarus, China, India, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Russia, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan |
| **ESAAMLG** | Eastern and Southern Africa | 1999 | Dar es Salaam, Tanzania | 19 member countries; intergovernmental body; studied wildlife poaching/illegal wildlife trade ML |
| **GABAC** | Central Africa | 2000 | Libreville, Gabon | Body of Economic and Monetary Community of Central Africa; FATF associate member since 2015 |

> **Key Exam Points -- FSRBs:**
> - There are 9 FSRBs, all FATF associate members
> - FATF is the only standard-setting body; FSRBs implement standards
> - Know each FSRB's region and acronym
> - APG is voluntary and cooperative; no treaty basis
> - CFATF originated from meetings in Aruba (1990) and Jamaica (1992)
> - MONEYVAL covers Council of Europe countries not in FATF

## 5. Other Influencing Bodies

### 5.1 Organization of American States / CICAD

- **OAS** became the first permanent international body to reach agreement on model AML legislation (May 1992, Nassau, Bahamas)
- **CICAD** (Inter-American Drug Abuse Control Commission): OAS entity focused on drug policy in the Western Hemisphere
- Contains an **Anti-Money Laundering Unit (CICAD-AMLU)** established in 1999
- Through the **Group of Experts**, develops **Model Regulations** on ML offenses related to drug trafficking and TF -- compatible with FATF 40 Recommendations
- Helped establish FIUs in Argentina, Chile, Ecuador, Brazil, Peru, Uruguay, Venezuela

### 5.2 Egmont Group of Financial Intelligence Units

- Founded in **1995** in Brussels (named for the Egmont-Arenberg Palace)
- Goal: provide a **forum for FIUs** worldwide to improve cooperation and securely share sensitive information
- Operates the **Egmont Secure Web (ESW)** for secure communication among FIUs
- Key functions: operational arm of international AML/CFT, secure exchange of expertise and financial intelligence, training, promoting FIU autonomy
- **FIUs should exchange information regardless of their status** (administrative, law enforcement, judicial, or other)
- Exchanged information used **only for the purpose** for which it was sought
- Published "FIUs in Action: 100 Cases" identifying **six most common ML indicators:**
  1. Large-scale cash transactions
  2. Atypical/uneconomical fund transfers to/from foreign jurisdictions
  3. Unusual business activities and transactions
  4. Large and/or rapid movements of funds
  5. Unrealistic wealth compared with client profile
  6. Defensive stance to questioning

### 5.3 The Wolfsberg Group

- **Association of global banks** focused on developing industry standards for KYC, AML, and CFT
- First met in **2000** at Wolfsberg castle in Switzerland with **Transparency International**
- **No enforcement powers**; issues voluntary guidelines
- Key publications:
  - **Anti-Money Laundering Principles for Private Banking** (2000, revised): Controls from basic CID to EDD; banks must accept only clients whose source of wealth/funds is reasonably legitimate; at least one person other than the private banker must approve new clients
  - **Principles on the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism**: Official terrorist lists, safe harbor immunity, EDD for high-risk sectors
  - **Correspondent Banking Principles** (2002, updated 2014): Risk-based and ongoing DD; considers geographic risk, ownership, customer base, products, regulatory status, AML controls, shell bank dealings, PEP involvement, downstream (nested) relationships
  - **Monitoring, Screening and Searching** guidance: Transaction monitoring frameworks, real-time screening, retroactive searches
  - **International Due Diligence Repository** (with Banker's Almanac, 2004): Standardized DD information including bylaws, licenses, annual reports, shareholder info, board bios, AML policies
- Prohibited use of **internal nonclient accounts** (concentration accounts) to hide client fund movements

### 5.4 The World Bank and the International Monetary Fund

- Since **2001**, require countries benefiting from financial/structural assistance to have effective ML controls
- Established collaborative framework with FATF using a **single global methodology** to assess compliance with FATF 40 Recommendations
- Assessments via **Financial Sector Assessment Program (FSAP)**, resulting in **Reports on the Observance of Standards and Codes (ROSCs)**
- In **2004**, permanently adopted pilot program assessing AML/CFT compliance, ending FATF's NCCT practice
- **2002**: Developed the *Reference Guide to Anti-Money Laundering and Combating the Financing of Terrorism*

> **Key Exam Points -- Other Bodies:**
> - OAS/CICAD: First to reach agreement on model AML legislation (1992); CICAD-AMLU provides technical assistance
> - Egmont Group: Forum for FIUs; founded 1995; uses Egmont Secure Web; six common ML indicators
> - Wolfsberg Group: Association of global banks; no enforcement power; voluntary principles for private banking, correspondent banking, CFT
> - World Bank/IMF: Require AML controls for financial assistance recipients; use single methodology with FATF; conduct assessments via FSAP/ROSCs

## 6. Key US Legislative and Regulatory Initiatives

### 6.1 USA PATRIOT Act (October 2001)

- Full name: Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act
- Strengthened BSA and ML laws to levels unseen since BSA (1970) and Money Laundering Control Act (1986)
- **Title III** = "The International Money Laundering Abatement and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act of 2001" -- contains most AML-related provisions
- Purpose: Increase strength of US measures to prevent, detect, and prosecute international ML and TF; subject foreign jurisdictions, FOs outside the US, and international transactions to special scrutiny
- Regulations compiled in **31 Code of Federal Regulation Chapter X**
- Applies to US and **non-US organizations doing business in the US**

#### Key Sections of the USA PATRIOT Act

**Section 311 -- Special Measures for Primary Money Laundering Concerns (31 U.S.C. 5318A)**
- Treasury can designate a foreign jurisdiction, FO, transaction type, or account type as a **"primary money laundering concern"**
- Can force US banks to halt financial dealings with the designee
- **Five special measures** (any or all):
  1. Recordkeeping/reporting on certain financial transactions
  2. Obtain beneficial ownership info on accounts by foreign persons
  3. Identify customers using a foreign bank's **payable-through account**
  4. Identify customers using a foreign bank's **correspondent account**
  5. **Close** certain payable-through and correspondent accounts
- Treasury Secretary must consult with Secretary of State and Attorney General before designating
- Distinct from **OFAC** designations (which are broader and trigger asset freezing)

**Section 312 -- Correspondent and Private Banking Accounts (31 U.S.C. 5318(i))**
- Requires DD and, in certain situations, **EDD for foreign correspondent accounts** and **private banking for non-US persons**
- DD program must be "appropriate, specific, and risk-based"
- Three DD measures: (1) Determine if EDD is necessary, (2) Assess ML risk, (3) Apply risk-based procedures
- **EDD required** for correspondent accounts of foreign banks operating under:
  - An **offshore banking license**
  - A license from a country designated **noncooperative** by an international organization
  - A license from a country designated as warranting **Section 311 special measures**
- **Private banking account** defined as: (a) minimum aggregate deposit of **US$1 million**, (b) for one or more **non-US persons**, (c) assigned to a bank employee acting as **liaison**
- Must ascertain identity of all beneficial owners, determine if any is a **senior foreign political figure** (PEP), ascertain source of funds, monitor for consistency

**Section 313 -- Prohibition on Correspondent Accounts for Foreign Shell Banks (31 U.S.C. 5318(j))**
- **Prohibits** US banks and securities brokers/dealers from maintaining correspondent accounts for **foreign shell banks** with no physical presence
- **Physical presence** = fixed address where the bank: is authorized to conduct banking, employs individuals full-time, maintains operating records, and is subject to inspection
- **Shell bank** does NOT include a regulated affiliate of a bank that maintains a physical presence
- Foreign banks must certify **every three years** they are not shell banks and do not permit shell bank access through nested correspondent relationships

**Sections 314(a) and 314(b) -- Information Sharing**
- Purpose: Help law enforcement identify, disrupt, and prevent terrorist acts and ML
- **314(a)**: Enables **law enforcement agencies** (federal, state, local, foreign/EU), through FinCEN, to reach 43,000+ points of contact at 22,000+ FOs to locate accounts of persons involved in terrorism or ML
- **314(b)**: Provides **safe harbor** for FOs to share information with each other (voluntarily) to identify and report ML/TF; must file notice with FinCEN; protects from liability for sharing

**Section 319(a) and (b) -- Forfeiture and Subpoena Powers**
- **319(a)**: Funds deposited at a **foreign bank** that has an **interbank account** in the US are deemed deposited in that interbank account -- allows US government to seize/forfeit funds
- **319(b)**: Attorney General or Treasury Secretary can **subpoena records** of any foreign bank that maintains a correspondent account in the US; can also subpoena the US correspondent bank for those records; **noncompliance** can lead to the **termination** of the correspondent relationship

**Section 326 -- Customer Identification Programs (CIPs)**
- Requires FOs to implement **reasonable procedures** to verify identity of persons opening accounts
- Minimum requirements: name, date of birth, address, identification number
- Must maintain records of verification for **5 years** after account closing
- Must check customer names against **government lists** of known/suspected terrorists or terrorist organizations

### 6.2 Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020 (AMLA)

- Part of the **National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021**
- Most significant AML reform since the USA PATRIOT Act
- Key provisions:
  - Established a **national beneficial ownership registry** (Corporate Transparency Act) -- companies must report beneficial owners to FinCEN
  - Expanded **BSA whistleblower protections** and incentives
  - Modernized AML/CFT framework to address **emerging threats** (cryptocurrency, cybercrime)
  - Enhanced **penalties** for BSA violations
  - Required studies on **de-risking**, trade-based ML, and other topics

### 6.3 Reach of US Criminal Money Laundering Laws

- US ML laws can have **extraterritorial reach**
- Criminal ML statutes: **18 U.S.C. 1956** (laundering of monetary instruments) and **18 U.S.C. 1957** (engaging in monetary transactions in property derived from specified unlawful activity)
- Apply when a transaction involves a US financial institution or takes place partly within the US

### 6.4 OFAC and Sanctions

- **Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)** -- part of the US Department of the Treasury
- Administers and enforces **economic and trade sanctions** based on US foreign policy and national security goals
- Maintains the **Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List (SDN List)**
- US persons (and in some cases non-US persons) are prohibited from dealing with SDN-listed individuals and entities
- Violations can result in **civil and criminal penalties**
- Section 311 actions are **distinct from** OFAC designations

> **Key Exam Points -- US Law:**
> - USA PATRIOT Act Title III is the primary AML section; applies to US and non-US orgs doing business in US
> - Section 311: Five special measures for "primary money laundering concerns"
> - Section 312: EDD for foreign correspondent accounts (offshore banks, noncooperative countries) and private banking (US$1M+ for non-US persons)
> - Section 313: Prohibits correspondent accounts for foreign shell banks; certification every 3 years
> - Section 314(a): Law enforcement to FO information requests via FinCEN
> - Section 314(b): Safe harbor for FO-to-FO voluntary information sharing
> - Section 319: Forfeiture of foreign bank funds via interbank accounts; subpoena power for foreign bank records
> - Section 326: CIP requirements -- name, DOB, address, ID number; records kept 5 years; check government lists
> - AMLA 2020: Beneficial ownership registry (Corporate Transparency Act), expanded whistleblower protections
> - OFAC: Administers sanctions, maintains SDN List; distinct from Section 311

| **Egmont Group** | Informal FIU network | Forum for FIUs; secure information exchange (ESW) |
| **OAS/CICAD** | Regional organization | Model AML regulations for the Americas |
| **Wolfsberg Group** | Association of global banks | Voluntary AML/KYC standards for private and correspondent banking |
| **World Bank/IMF** | International financial institutions | Assess compliance via FSAP/ROSCs; require AML for financial assistance |
| **OFAC** | US Treasury office | Administers US economic sanctions; SDN List |
| **FinCEN** | US Treasury bureau | US FIU; administers BSA; facilitates 314(a) and 314(b) |
