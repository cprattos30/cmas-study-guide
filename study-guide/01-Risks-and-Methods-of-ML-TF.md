## 1. Overview of Money Laundering

### What Is Money Laundering?

- **Money laundering** is the process by which criminals disguise the **original ownership and control** of the proceeds of criminal conduct by making such proceeds appear to have derived from a legitimate source
- ML allows criminals to **hide and legitimize** proceeds derived from illegal activities
- It is a **global problem** that affects virtually every country and financial system
- Estimated that **2-5% of global GDP** is laundered annually (hundreds of billions to over a trillion USD)
- Without the ability to launder money, criminals cannot effectively use or enjoy their illicit profits

### The Three Stages of Money Laundering

- **Placement** -- the physical introduction of bulk cash into the financial system
  - Most **vulnerable stage** for detection because cash is bulky and hard to explain
  - Methods: bank deposits, purchasing monetary instruments, commingling with legitimate business funds, currency smuggling, using casinos
  - Often involves **structuring** (breaking large amounts into smaller deposits)

- **Layering** -- separating the illicit proceeds from their source through layers of complex financial transactions
  - Purpose: create **distance** between the funds and the crime
  - Methods: wire transfers between multiple accounts/jurisdictions, converting cash into monetary instruments, purchasing/selling investments, shell company transactions
  - Goal is to make the audit trail as **complex and confusing** as possible

- **Integration** -- providing an apparently legitimate explanation for the illicit proceeds
  - Funds re-enter the economy appearing **"clean"**
  - Methods: real estate purchases, luxury assets, business investments, loan-back schemes, false import/export invoices
  - Most **difficult stage to detect** because funds appear legitimate

### Economic and Social Consequences of ML

- **Undermines the integrity** of financial institutions and systems
- **Distorts markets** and creates unfair competition (criminals can offer below-market prices)
- Causes **loss of tax revenue** for governments
- Can destabilize entire **economies**, particularly in developing countries
- Creates **reputational risk** for countries and institutions
- Facilitates **corruption** and erodes the rule of law
- Weakens **public trust** in financial systems
- Can lead to **economic policy distortion** when laundered money flows are significant

### AML/CFT Programs and Individual Accountability

- Financial institutions must establish **AML/CFT compliance programs** to detect and prevent ML/TF
- Key components: internal policies/procedures, designated compliance officer, ongoing training, independent audit/testing
- **Individual accountability** is increasing -- compliance officers, senior management, and employees can face personal **criminal and civil liability**
- Willful blindness or deliberate ignorance is **not a defense**
- Organizations face **fines, sanctions, loss of license**, and reputational damage for failures

### Methods of Money Laundering

- **Structuring/Smurfing** -- breaking large sums into smaller deposits below reporting thresholds
- **Currency smuggling** -- physically moving cash across borders
- **Using intermediaries** (nominees, straw men) to conduct transactions
- **Commingling** -- mixing illicit funds with legitimate business revenue
- **Shell companies** -- using entities with no real business to layer transactions
- **Trade-based ML** -- manipulating trade invoices (over/under-invoicing)
- **Real estate** -- purchasing properties with illicit funds
- **Cash-intensive businesses** -- restaurants, car washes, etc., used to explain large cash deposits

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Know the three stages cold -- Placement, Layering, Integration -- and be able to identify which stage an activity falls into in a scenario question.
> - Placement is the most vulnerable to detection; Integration is the hardest to detect.
> - ML estimated at 2-5% of global GDP.
> - Individual accountability is a growing trend -- compliance officers face personal liability.

## 2. Risks in Banks and Depository Institutions

### Electronic Transfer of Funds (Wire Transfers)

- Wire transfers are one of the **most used methods** to layer illicit funds
- They provide **speed, distance, and minimal audit trail** between criminal and proceeds
- Criminals exploit the **volume** of daily wire transfers to hide illicit ones
- Red flags: wire transfers to/from high-risk jurisdictions, frequent round-dollar amounts, no apparent business purpose, transfers immediately sent to third parties
- **Cover payments** can obscure originator/beneficiary information in correspondent banking chains

### Remote Deposit Capture (RDC)

- Allows customers to deposit checks **electronically** by scanning them from a remote location
- Risks: eliminates face-to-face contact, difficult to verify identity, potential for **duplicate deposits**, use of fraudulent or stolen checks
- Can be used to deposit checks at **multiple institutions simultaneously**

### Correspondent Banking

- A **correspondent bank** provides services on behalf of another financial institution (the **respondent bank**)
- Services include wire transfers, cash management, check clearing, and foreign exchange
- **High risk** because the correspondent bank processes transactions for customers it does not directly know
- **Nested relationships** (a respondent bank providing sub-correspondent services to other banks) increase risk significantly
- Correspondent bank relies on the respondent bank's **AML/CFT controls**
- Must conduct due diligence on respondent banks: understand their AML program, customer base, regulatory environment

### Payable-Through Accounts (PTAs)

- Accounts maintained at a correspondent bank that allow customers of a **foreign bank to directly access** the correspondent bank's services
- Foreign bank's customers can conduct transactions **as if they were direct customers** of the correspondent bank
- Extremely high risk because the correspondent bank has **no direct relationship** with the underlying customers
- Correspondent bank relies entirely on the foreign bank's **KYC/CDD** procedures

### Concentration Accounts

- Also known as **omnibus, suspense, settlement, or intraday accounts**
- Used internally by banks to **aggregate funds from multiple sources** for processing
- Risks: can obscure the **identity of individual transactors** and the audit trail
- Customer names and identifying information may not accompany transactions through these accounts
- Controls needed: prohibit direct customer access, maintain adequate transaction records, reconcile frequently

### Private Banking

- Provides **personalized, high-level financial services** to high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs)
- Higher risk due to: high transaction volumes, personalized service (relationship managers may overlook red flags), complex products, cross-border transactions, use of trusts and corporate vehicles
- **Commission-based compensation** for relationship managers creates incentive to overlook suspicious activity
- Relationship managers may develop **personal loyalty** to clients that conflicts with compliance duties
- Must apply **enhanced due diligence (EDD)** and ongoing monitoring

### Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs)

- **PEPs** are individuals who hold or have held prominent **public positions** (heads of state, senior politicians, judicial/military officials, senior executives of state-owned enterprises, and their **family members and close associates**)
- PEPs represent **higher risk** because of their potential to abuse public office for private gain (corruption, bribery, embezzlement)
- Institutions must: **identify** PEPs, obtain **senior management approval** for the relationship, determine **source of wealth and funds**, conduct **enhanced ongoing monitoring**
- PEP status is **not automatic grounds for rejection** -- it triggers EDD requirements
- Both **domestic and foreign** PEPs require attention

### Structuring and Microstructuring

- **Structuring** -- deliberately breaking transactions into amounts below the **reporting threshold** (e.g., below $10,000 in the US) to avoid triggering a Currency Transaction Report (CTR)
- Also called **"smurfing"** when multiple people make deposits
- **Microstructuring** -- structuring at even smaller amounts to avoid detection by monitoring systems
- Structuring is **illegal** in itself (even with legitimately earned funds) in many jurisdictions
- Red flags: multiple transactions just below thresholds, multiple branches same day, multiple people depositing into the same account

### Credit Unions

- Smaller institutions that may have **fewer resources** for AML/CFT compliance
- Subject to the **same AML requirements** as banks
- Vulnerable because of community/membership-based culture that may discourage questioning members
- May have **less sophisticated** transaction monitoring systems

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Correspondent banking risk increases with nested relationships and PTAs.
> - Concentration accounts risk: loss of audit trail when customer identity is stripped from transactions.
> - PEPs require EDD, senior management approval, and source-of-wealth determination -- but PEP status alone does not mean the person is a criminal.
> - Structuring is illegal even with legitimate funds. It is a crime to deliberately avoid reporting thresholds.
> - Private banking risk is heightened by relationship manager conflicts of interest and commission-based pay.

## 3. Nonbank Financial Institutions

### Credit Cards

- Can be used for ML through: **overpayment** and requesting a refund check, purchasing high-value items for resale, balance transfers between accounts
- Red flags: payments from third parties, payments from foreign jurisdictions, cash advances with immediate repayment, overpayment followed by refund requests
- **Third-party payment processors** (TPPPs) process card transactions for merchants and may have weak AML controls
- TPPPs are high risk because the credit card company/bank may not have a **direct relationship** with the underlying merchant

### Money Services Businesses (MSBs)

- Include: **money transmitters, check cashers, currency exchangers, sellers of money orders/traveler's checks, prepaid card providers**
- MSBs can be categorized as **principals** (provide MSB services directly) or **agents** (offer MSB-type services alongside their primary business, e.g., grocery stores)
- **Principal MSBs** must have: written AML policies, BSA officer, training, independent audits, suspicious activity monitoring
- **Agents must follow the same regulations** as principal MSBs
- Vulnerable due to: **cash-intensive** nature, transactional customer relationships, serving underserved/unbanked populations, some operating solely online
- Banks must conduct due diligence on MSB customers to confirm they have **adequate AML/CFT programs** and are properly licensed/registered
- **Case -- Bangladesh Bank Heist (2016):** Three MSBs including Philrem facilitated movement of US$81 million from the heist via remittances from fictitious accounts to Philippine casinos

### Insurance Companies

- **Life insurance and annuities** are the highest-risk insurance products for ML (per FATF)
- Property, casualty, title, and health insurance are **lower risk** (no investment features, cash buildups, or fund transfer options)
- Vulnerable features: **cash surrender values, single premium payments, ability to overfund, free-look period cancellation, lump-sum payouts**
- Products with **no cash surrender value** are the **least attractive** to launderers
- Red flags: customer more interested in **cancellation terms** than benefits, early surrender with penalty acceptance, third-party premium payments, large single premiums
- ML methods: overfund policy then withdraw (accepting penalty), purchase and redeem single-premium insurance bonds at a discount, use free-look period to get a clean check
- Vulnerabilities: **intermediaries** (agents/brokers) have great autonomy, sales-driven culture may overlook red flags, noncaptive agents work across multiple companies
- Insurance companies must consider whether they allow: **cash/cash equivalents** to purchase products, single premium/lump-sum payments, borrowing against policy value

### Securities Broker-Dealers

- Capital markets are **vast in size**, dwarfing deposit banking
- Securities typically used in **layering stage** (after cash has been placed in the system)
- Little currency involved -- runs on **electronic transfers and paper**
- Aspects increasing ML exposure: international nature, speed, free-of-payment asset transfers, ease of converting to cash, routine wire transfers across jurisdictions, commission-driven environment, use of **nominee/trustee accounts**
- ML methods: **wash trading** (buying and selling same security to create illusion of activity), mirror trades, accounts used as depositories not for trading, offsetting transactions
- FATF identifies greatest vulnerabilities in: wholesale markets, unregulated funds, wealth management, investment funds, bearer securities, bills of exchange
- Red flags: customer liquidates all assets abruptly, unconcerned about losses/commissions, deposits physical securities in large quantities, penny stock activity
- In the US, **SEC and FINRA** oversee broker-dealer AML requirements
- **Case -- Deutsche Bank Mirror Trades (2017):** FCA fined Deutsche Bank GBP 163 million for AML/CFT failures. DB Moscow executed 2,400+ pairs of mirror trades (2012-2014), transferring US$6 billion from Russia through London to Cyprus/Estonia/Latvia. Deficiencies included inadequate KYC/CDD, flawed risk-rating, no automated AML detection systems.
- **Case -- Guotai Junan Securities (2020):** Hong Kong SFC fined HK$25.2 million for AML failures related to 15,600 third-party deposits/withdrawals totaling HK$37.5 billion. Failed to identify red flags, perform KYC on third parties, or ascertain source of funds.

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Life insurance and annuities are highest-risk insurance products; policies with no cash surrender value are lowest risk.
> - A customer asking about cancellation terms rather than benefits is a classic insurance ML red flag.
> - MSB agents must follow the same AML/CFT regulations as principal MSBs.
> - Banks must verify MSBs have adequate AML programs before establishing relationships.
> - Securities are primarily used in the layering stage; wash trading and mirror trades are key typologies.
> - TPPPs are high risk because the bank lacks a direct relationship with the underlying merchants.

## 4. Nonfinancial Businesses and Professions

### Casinos and Gaming

- Among the most **cash-intensive** businesses; billions in cash flow between customers and house
- ML through casinos generally occurs during **placement and layering** stages
- Methods: buy chips with cash, engage in **minimal play**, redeem chips for a casino check ("clean" funds); use casino credit lines and repay with illicit funds; transfer credit between casino locations in different jurisdictions
- **Junkets** (casino-based tourism) present significant risk -- junket operators move funds across borders through multiple casinos, obscuring source and ownership
- Online gambling additional risks: transactions through credit/debit cards, typically unregulated offshore operators, use of prepaid cards/virtual currency/peer-to-peer transfers
- Red flags per FinCEN/FATF:
  - Attempts to **structure** transactions below reporting thresholds
  - Using the casino cage as a **bank** (deposits, wire transfers, minimal gaming)
  - **Minimal gaming** with large chip purchases then redemption for check
  - **Bill stuffing** (inserting small bills into slot machines, minimal play, redeeming for large bills)
  - Betting **both sides** of an even bet (hedging)
  - Requesting checks payable to **third parties**
- FinCEN expects casinos to know the **source of high rollers' funds**
- **Case -- Crown Melbourne (2019):** ILGA investigation found Crown actively facilitated ML for wealthy Chinese gamblers since 2014, using shell companies, junket operators from Macau, and accepted large cash deposits at VIP cashier desks. Crown staff aggregated deposits to hide from AML team. Nearly 700 deposits made below reporting thresholds.
- Enforcement examples: Tinian Dynasty ($75M fine), Caesars Palace ($9.5M), Sparks Nugget ($1M), Artichoke Joe's ($8M)

### Dealers in High-Value Items (Precious Metals, Jewelry, Art)

- **USA PATRIOT Act** requires certain dealers in precious metals, stones, and jewels to establish AML programs
- Many jurisdictions do **not regulate** these industries for ML control
- **Gold** is extremely attractive: high intrinsic value, compact, easily transported, holds value regardless of form, cultural significance, can be melted and reformed
- **Diamonds** used in conflict zones by terrorist groups; purchased directly with illicit funds
- **False invoicing** is common -- transactions may not actually occur but paperwork justifies fund transfers
- **Precious metal pool accounts** maintained by large companies can be used to transfer value between persons
- **Art market** vulnerabilities: anonymity in transactions, subjective pricing, use of intermediaries, offshore accounts, international markets, historically unregulated
- US Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020 designated art markets as **high-risk environments**
- EU Fifth Directive treats art market participants **same as financial institutions** (must register, perform CDD, take risk-based approach)
- **Case -- Operation Apex (2020):** Wu gang laundered drug/wildlife trafficking proceeds through gold, precious metals, and diamonds across US/Mexico/Hong Kong using fake invoices and false documentation
- **Case -- Elemetal LLC (2018):** US gold refinery forfeited $15M and pled guilty to failing to maintain adequate AML -- did not perform KYC/CDD/EDD on gold suppliers from Central/South America
- **Case -- Philip Rivkin (2016):** Convicted of laundering $78M through art purchases (over 2,000 pieces) from biodiesel fraud proceeds

### Travel Agencies

- Used to **mix illegal funds with clean money** -- high-priced airline tickets, hotels, vacation expenses
- ML methods: purchasing expensive tickets for another person who then requests a refund; structured wire transfers for installment payments; establishing tour operator networks with false bookings
- Can be used in **all three stages** of ML
- Cash-intensive businesses that can **commingle** legitimate and illegitimate funds
- Also used by terrorists to **facilitate travel logistics** and enter false identities into travel arrangements
- **Case -- ISIS Travel Agency (2015):** Indonesian militant Gigih Rahmat Dewe opened a travel agency on Bintan Island as cover for ISIS, funded by an ISIS operative, to facilitate safe terrorist travel, provide ML cover, and generate revenue

### Vehicle Sales

- Includes automobiles, trucks, motorcycles, aircraft, boats, ships
- Common element: **unreported use of currency** to pay for vehicles
- ML methods: structured cash deposits below thresholds, sequential checks/money orders, buying and selling vehicles to create complex transaction layers, third-party payments
- **Down-trading** scheme: trade in expensive vehicle for cheaper one, receive difference by check
- **Case -- Leontaritis (2019):** Luxury car dealer sentenced to 20 years for laundering drug money through Vanderhall Exotics of Houston, accepting cash from known drug dealers, using fraudulent invoices to hide buyer identities

### Gatekeepers: Lawyers, Accountants, Notaries, Auditors

- Professionals who can either **block or facilitate** entry of illegitimate money into the financial system
- Responsibilities include: identifying clients, CDD, maintaining records, reporting suspicious activity
- **Cannot "tip off"** clients who are subjects of SARs
- Criminals use legal professionals to provide a **veneer of respectability** and create additional steps in investigation chains
- FATF identified functions most useful to launderers:
  - **Creating/managing corporate vehicles** and trusts
  - **Buying/selling property** (cover for layering/integration)
  - Performing **financial transactions** (deposits, withdrawals, wire transfers)
  - Providing **financial/tax advice**
  - Providing **introductions** to financial organizations
  - Setting up and managing **charities**
- FATF's **five categories of red flag indicators**: (1) Characteristics of the client, (2) Characteristics of involved parties, (3) Characteristics of source of funds, (4) Characteristics of the lawyer, (5) Characteristics of the retainer
- Attorney-client privilege creates tension with AML/CFT obligations -- various alternatives debated
- In EU countries, **mandatory AML duties** apply to gatekeepers under FATF Recommendations
- **Case -- Ross McKay (2019):** UK lawyer sentenced to 7 years for helping organized criminals launder money through 80+ mortgage fraud transactions, building a GBP 10.8M property empire using nominee names and disguised fund sources
- **Case -- Special Skills:** Attorney convicted of conspiracy to launder drug proceeds by forming corporation in client's wife's name, arranging fake construction contracts, and advising on avoiding reporting requirements

### Investment and Commodity Advisors

- **Commodity trading advisors (CTAs)** advise on futures contracts, commodity options, swaps
- Key definitions: **commodities** (food, grains, metals), **futures contracts** (buy/sell at future date), **options** (right but not obligation), **commodity pool** (combined investor funds), **omnibus accounts** (combined transactions, identities unknown)
- ML vulnerabilities: transfers to unrelated accounts/high-risk jurisdictions, frequent additions/withdrawals, third-party checks/wires, anonymous custodial arrangements

### Trust and Company Service Providers (TCSPs)

- Gatekeepers who set up trusts and provide legal/administrative functions for corporations
- TCSP services include: acting as agent to **form companies**, serving as **director/secretary**, providing **registered office/address**, acting as **trustee**, acting as **nominee shareholder**
- Must understand **source of funds** and the **general purpose** of structures created
- Must always identify **beneficial owners** and controlling persons
- Vulnerabilities: inconsistent regulatory application, limited market restrictions, potential to operate unlicensed, CDD may be delegated to other FIs
- ML indicators: complex/opaque legal entities, consultancy fees to shell companies in secrecy jurisdictions, use of TCSPs in jurisdictions with weak AML/CFT laws, multiple intercompany loans without apparent purpose

### Real Estate

- **Frequently used** for ML -- a classic method of laundering illicit funds
- Attractive because: can be purchased with cash, beneficial ownership can be disguised, stable/reliable investment, value can increase through improvements
- **Escrow accounts** facilitate ML due to high volumes and diverse transactions flowing through them
- ML methods:
  - **Undervaluation** -- purchase property below market value, pay difference in unreported cash, later sell at full value
  - **Loan-back** -- criminal gives associate illicit money, associate provides a "loan" or "mortgage" back to the criminal with proper documentation
  - Structuring cash deposits for down payments
  - Using **shell companies/trusts** to hide beneficial ownership
  - Third-party straw buyers ("**cleanskins**" in Australia)
  - Generating **rental income** to legitimize funds
- Red flags: cash used for purchase/down payment/loan repayment, multiple purchases/sales in short periods, use of offshore lenders, unknown source of foreign wires, property ownership as sole link to the country
- **GTOs (Geographic Targeting Orders):** FinCEN requires US title insurance companies to identify **natural persons behind shell companies** making all-cash high-end residential real estate purchases in specified metro areas
- **NYT "Towers of Secrecy" (2015):** Found nearly half of most expensive US residential properties purchased through shell companies

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Casino ML primarily occurs during placement and layering. Bill stuffing, minimal play, and using the cage as a bank are key red flags.
> - Gatekeepers provide a veneer of respectability. FATF identifies five categories of red flags for legal professionals.
> - TCSPs must identify beneficial owners and understand source of funds and purpose of structures.
> - Real estate ML methods to know: loan-back scheme, undervaluation, use of shell companies, GTOs.
> - Gold is extremely attractive for ML: compact, holds value, easily transported, can be exchanged for cash globally.
> - Art markets are now designated high-risk by the US AML Act of 2020 and the EU Fifth Directive.

## 5. International Trade Activity

### Free Trade Zones (FTZs)

- **Designated geographic areas** with special regulatory and tax treatments for trade-related goods and services
- Often located near **ports of entry** in developing countries, also in regional financial centers
- Examples: **Colon Free Trade Zone** (Panama), **Shanghai Free Trade Zone** (China)
- FATF systemic weaknesses in FTZs:
  - Inadequate **AML/CFT safeguards**
  - Minimal **oversight** by local authorities
  - Weak procedures to **inspect goods** and legal entities
  - Inadequate **recordkeeping** and IT systems
  - Lack of **cooperation** between FTZs and local customs
- Some FTZs are as **large as cities**, making monitoring of incoming/outgoing cargo extremely difficult
- FTZs provide an opportune setting for **trade-based money laundering** schemes

### Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)

- FATF defines TBML as **disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value by using trade transactions to legitimize their illicit origins**
- Achieved through **misrepresentation of the price, quantity, or quality** of imports and exports
- TBML is **increasingly attractive** as controls on other ML methods improve
- **Six methods of executing TBML:**
  1. **Overinvoicing/Underinvoicing** -- invoicing above or below fair market price to transfer value between buyer and seller
  2. **Overshipping/Short-shipping** -- difference between invoiced and shipped quantity creates excess value
  3. **Ghost-shipping** -- fictitious trades with documentation but no actual goods shipped
  4. **Shell companies** -- reduce transparency of ownership in transactions
  5. **Multiple invoicing** -- numerous invoices for the same shipment, justifying multiple payments
  6. **Black market trades** -- domestic fund transfer used to pay for goods by a foreign importer (BMPE)
- **Letters of credit** can facilitate TBML by creating appearance of legitimate import transactions
- **Funnel accounts** receive multiple cash deposits below reporting thresholds in one area, funds withdrawn in a different area quickly -- linked to TBML and Mexico-related criminal organizations
- **Case -- EU TBML Investigation (2017):** Europol-supported investigation of Italian OCG using network of shell companies to launder drug proceeds through luxury vehicle purchases in Germany, fake VAT chains, and export/import of goods across multiple EU jurisdictions

### Black Market Peso Exchange (BMPE)

- A form of TBML where **drug dollars in the US** are purchased by Colombian peso brokers
- Brokers deposit dollars in US bank accounts, sell checks/wires to **legitimate businesses in Mexico/Colombia** that need dollars to buy US goods
- Goods are shipped to Mexico/Colombia, sold for pesos, pesos paid to the drug trafficker
- Originated in the 1950s by Colombian importers avoiding taxes; adopted by drug cartels in the 1970s
- **BMPE has evolved** beyond the original Colombian model due to increased US bank diligence
- Now involves: **structured deposits, money orders, bulk cash smuggling** to foreign institutions (Mexico, Central/South America), wired back as payments for trade goods
- **Case -- Agarwal Electronics (2021):** CEO of US wholesale electronics company pled guilty to conspiracy involving unlicensed MSB operating a BMPE scheme between Colombia and the US, using his bank account to receive drug proceeds and arrange electronics exports to Colombian suppliers who paid in pesos to money brokers
- **Complex BMPE (2015):** FinCEN issued GTOs for ~700 Miami electronics exporters to disrupt sophisticated TBML/BMPE schemes used by Sinaloa and Los Zetas cartels

### Wildlife Trafficking

- **Illegal trade, smuggling, poaching, capture, and collection** of endangered species and protected wildlife
- Affects more than **7,000 species** worldwide
- Three stages: (1) **Source** -- poaching jurisdiction, often involves bribing corrupt officials; (2) **Transit** -- movement of disguised goods, exploiting customs weaknesses; (3) **Destination** -- final sale, often via in-person or online black markets
- Profits are **lowest at source** and **highest at destination** (distributors)
- Organizations can combat wildlife trafficking by: joining the **United for Wildlife Financial Taskforce**, using SAR mechanisms, reviewing **FATF's Money Laundering and the Illegal Wildlife Trade** report

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Know all six TBML methods: overinvoicing, underinvoicing, overshipping/short-shipping, ghost-shipping, shell companies, multiple invoicing, black market trades.
> - BMPE flow: Drug dollars (US) -> peso broker -> US bank accounts -> legitimate businesses buy US goods -> goods sold in Colombia for pesos -> pesos to drug trafficker.
> - FTZs are high-risk due to minimal oversight, weak AML safeguards, and inadequate inspection procedures.
> - Funnel accounts: deposits below thresholds in one area, rapidly withdrawn in another area.
> - TBML is increasingly attractive as traditional ML methods face greater scrutiny.

## 6. New Payment Products and Services

### Prepaid Cards

- Have same characteristics that make **cash attractive**: portable, valuable, exchangeable, anonymous
- Types: **open-loop** (network-branded like Visa/MC, usable anywhere) vs. **closed-loop** (specific merchant/retailer only)
- Can be **reloadable** or **nonreloadable**
- FATF risk factors: anonymous cardholders, anonymous funding, anonymous access to funds, **high value limits**, no limit on number of cards, **global ATM access**, offshore issuers, substitute for bulk-cash smuggling
- After the **2015 Paris attacks**, the EU reduced limits under the Fourth and Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directives
- EU Fifth Directive: prepaid cards not subject to CDD limited to **EUR 150** maximum; online transactions limited to **EUR 50**

### Mobile Payments and Electronic Money

- **E-money** is a prepaid means of payment that can make payments to multiple persons, which are distinct legal or natural entities
- Products can be card-based, app-based, or online account-based
- Risk factors: high transaction/purse limits, multiple purses/cards per customer, complex "white-label" business models with outsourcing, funding with unverified sources/cash, non-face-to-face transactions
- Controls: limits on storage/transactions/turnover, transaction monitoring systems, geolocation/device/IP tracking, cooperation with merchants, geographic restrictions
- **CDD** is an important control for e-money -- some simplified due diligence allowed for low-risk/low-value scenarios

### Virtual Currency / Cryptocurrency

- **Virtual currency (VC)** is a medium of exchange, unit of account, and/or store of value operating **without legal tender status**
- Two categories: **centralized** (single administrator, e.g., Bahamian Sand Dollar) and **decentralized** (no central authority, e.g., Bitcoin)
- FATF distinguishes: **convertible** VCs (can be exchanged for fiat, e.g., Bitcoin, Ethereum, Ripple) vs. **non-convertible** (limited to specific domain, e.g., gaming coins)
- FinCEN categorizes participants: **User** (obtains VC to buy goods/services), **Exchanger** (business exchanging VC for real currency), **Administrator** (business issuing/redeeming VC)
- **Exchangers and Administrators** are MSBs engaging in money transmission -- must comply with AML registration, reporting, recordkeeping
- FATF amended Recommendations to require **virtual asset service providers (VASPs)** to be regulated for AML/CFT (Recommendation 15)
- **"Travel rule"** (Recommendation 16) expanded to cover VC transactions -- requires exchange of identifying information between originators and beneficiaries
- As of 2021, **70 of 128 jurisdictions** (55%) had not yet implemented revised FATF Recommendations into national law
- Key risks: **anonymity** (especially privacy coins like Monero), speed of transactions across borders, use of **mixers/tumblers** to obscure transaction trails, peer-to-peer exchanges, DeFi platforms
- Most common VC misuse: sales of **illicit substances** on the dark web, followed by fraud/scams/ransomware/extortion
- **Case -- BTC-e (2017):** US authorities seized one of the world's largest crypto exchanges. Founder Alexander Vinnik arrested for laundering US$4 billion in bitcoin. Users operated with high anonymity, could obscure transactions, and openly discussed criminality on platform chat. Vinnik sentenced to 5 years in France.

### Dark Web

- Uses **anonymizing software** (e.g., Tor) to enable untraceable internet activity
- Cryptocurrency is the **preferred payment method** on dark web marketplaces
- Common pattern: sellers list on dark web, accept crypto prepayment, ship drugs via regular mail
- **Operation DisrupTor (2020):** International operation by US JCODE and Europol arrested 179 darknet drug traffickers, seized US$6.5M in cash/crypto, 500 kg of drugs, 63 firearms
- Criminals are increasingly moving to **encrypted chat networks** as law enforcement identifies darknet operators

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Open-loop prepaid cards (network-branded) are higher risk than closed-loop (merchant-specific).
> - FinCEN classifies VC participants as Users, Exchangers, and Administrators. Exchangers and Administrators are MSBs.
> - FATF Recommendation 15 requires VASPs to be regulated; Recommendation 16 ("travel rule") applies to VC transactions.
> - Privacy coins (Monero) ensure all users are anonymous, unlike Bitcoin which has a public ledger.
> - The dark web relies on cryptocurrency for anonymity; Operation DisrupTor was a landmark international enforcement action.
> - Know the difference between centralized vs. decentralized and convertible vs. non-convertible VCs.

## 7. Corporate Vehicles Used to Facilitate Illicit Finance

### Public and Private Companies

- **Public companies:** shares publicly traded, significant regulation, board/ownership information publicly available -- **lower ML risk**
- **Private limited companies:** not publicly traded, restrictive share numbers, minimal regulatory oversight -- **higher ML risk**
- **Limited liability companies (LLCs):** can be owned/managed **anonymously**, by anyone including foreign persons and business entities -- very attractive for ML
- **International business corporations (IBCs):** formed outside the person's country of residence, typically in offshore/tax haven jurisdictions, reduce transparency, often use local nominee owners/directors

### Bearer Shares in Corporate Formation

- **Bearer bonds, stock certificates, and shares** belong to whoever physically holds them -- **no registry of owners**
- Prime ML vehicles because transfer requires only **physical handover** of the certificate
- FATF Recommendation: FIs should conduct **EDD** and ask questions about beneficial ownership before issuing, accepting, or creating bearer shares/trusts
- **Bearer checks** are negotiable instruments paid to the **holder** rather than a named payee -- no identity verification required below certain thresholds

### Shell and Shelf Companies

- **Shell company:** a company that at incorporation has **no significant assets or operations** -- legally registered but no legitimate business
- **Shelf company:** a corporation created with **no activity**, "put on the shelf," and later **sold** to someone who prefers a pre-existing corporation
- Often purchased from **lawyers, accountants, secretarial companies**
- Stock may be issued in **bearer shares** (whoever carries them is the owner)
- **Tax haven countries** with strict secrecy laws further conceal ownership
- Four purposes for shell companies in ML (per Canadian report):
  1. **Convert** cash proceeds of crime into alternative assets
  2. **Create perception** that illicit funds came from a legitimate source (commingle with cash-intensive businesses like restaurants, bars, gas stations)
  3. **Facilitate** fake business transactions (loans between controlled companies, fictitious expenses/salaries, false invoicing, purchasing real estate)
  4. **Conceal criminal ownership** (using nominees as owners/directors/officers, incorporating as subsidiaries of foreign companies in secrecy jurisdictions)
- **Front businesses** differ from shell companies in that they **actually operate legitimately** while also laundering money

### Trusts

- Private **fiduciary arrangements** where a **grantor/settlor** places assets with a **trustee** for future distribution to **beneficiaries**
- Two types: **revocable** (grantor can terminate) and **irrevocable** (cannot be terminated once created)
- **"Remaindermen"** receive residual assets after the trust's term concludes
- ML significance of trusts: convert illicit cash into less suspicious assets, disguise criminal ownership, link between different laundering vehicles (real estate, shell companies, nominees)
- **Asset protection trusts (APTs):** irrevocable trusts settled offshore to protect wealth from creditors; can be moved between jurisdictions to avoid court orders
- Trust payments to beneficiaries are useful for ML because they do **not need justification** as compensation or asset transfers
- **Lawyers' trust accounts** (also called IOLTA accounts) can hold money "in trust" for clients -- illicit money may be placed in these accounts under the name of a client, nominee, or company

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - Shell company = no assets/operations at incorporation. Shelf company = pre-created with no activity, sold later.
> - Bearer shares transfer ownership by physical handover with no registry -- a major ML risk.
> - LLCs are attractive for ML because they can be owned/managed anonymously by anyone, including foreign persons.
> - Know the four purposes of shell companies: convert cash, create legitimate appearance, facilitate fake transactions, conceal ownership.
> - Trusts can be used at every stage of ML. APTs settled offshore can be moved between jurisdictions to avoid legal action.
> - Front businesses differ from shell companies by actually operating a legitimate business while commingling illicit funds.

## 8. Terrorist Financing

### Overview

- After **9/11**, G-7 urged freezing of known terrorist assets; FATF issued Special Recommendations on terrorist financing (now incorporated into the 40 Recommendations)
- **Recommendation 5** encourages countries to criminalize terrorist financing and ensure it is a designated **ML predicate offense**
- Cutting off financial support is **essential to disrupting** terrorist operations and preventing attacks

### Differences Between Terrorist Financing and Money Laundering

| | **Money Laundering** | **Terrorist Financing** |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin of funds** | Always from **illegal** activity | Can be from **legal or illegal** sources |
| **Purpose** | Make illegal funds **usable legally** | Fund **illegal political purpose** (terrorism) |
| **Beneficiaries** | Usually the individuals who committed the predicate crime | Not typically the individuals who raised the funds; benefits **terrorist activity** |
| **Amounts** | Often **large** sums | Can involve **very small** amounts |
| **Direction** | "Dirty" money made to look "clean" | "Clean" or "dirty" money used for **illicit purpose** |

- Key similarity: both use **similar methods** -- cash smuggling, structuring, monetary instruments, wire transfers, debit/credit/prepaid cards, hawala
- Terrorist groups also need to disguise the link between themselves and their **legitimate funding sources** to ensure continued future use
- Over **half** of individuals investigated by US law enforcement for terrorist ties had engaged in suspected ML, including structuring

### Detecting Terrorist Financing

- **9/11 Commission** found that neither hijackers nor their facilitators were financial system experts, but they blended in enough to avoid detection
- Pre-9/11 ML controls focused on **drug trafficking and large-scale fraud**, not the small-dollar transactions terrorists used
- Organizations must consider: accounts used as **fronts** for persons with terrorist links, names on **terrorist lists**, frequent large cash deposits in **NPO accounts**, high transaction volumes, mismatch between banking activity and business nature
- FATF recommends attention to classic ML indicators: **dormant/low-sum accounts** suddenly receiving wire transfers followed by daily cash withdrawals, customer non-cooperation
- **Case -- The Maute Group:** 2018 Analyst Exchange Program (AEP) was a multinational project (Australia, Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia) to identify money-moving networks financing the radical Islamist Maute Group (trained by ISIS). Funded by the Maute family, ISIS, drug sales, and anonymous mobile payments.

### How Terrorists Raise, Move, and Store Funds

- **State sponsorship** has decreased due to global sanctions; terrorists now seek supplemental income
- Terrorist funding sources include: oil trade, **extortion**, cash couriers, **kidnapping for ransom**, trafficking of humans and arms, **racketeering**, drug trade, charitable abuse
- Methods of moving funds: formal banking, **hawala/IVTS**, cash smuggling, trade, cryptocurrency, mobile payments, abuse of NPOs/charities

### Hawala and Informal Value Transfer Systems (IVTS)

- **Hawala** is an informal value-transfer system involving international transfer of value **outside the legitimate banking system**
- Based on **trust** and a network of **hawaladars** (brokers)
- Also known as: **"hundi"** (Hindi), **"chiti banking"** (China), **"poey kuan"** (Thailand), **"chop shop banking"** (China)
- How it works: sender deposits funds with a hawaladar in Country A; hawaladar contacts counterpart in Country B; counterpart makes funds available to recipient; hawaladars settle through normal trade or other means
- Typically **no physical movement of currency** -- transfer confirmed through coded information (chits, text messages, emails, online chat)
- Legitimate uses: immigrants sending remittances (less expensive, faster, no bank account needed, cultural preference)
- ML/TF risks: operates **without government supervision**, leaves **little or no paper trail**, customer details communicated by phone/fax/email only
- Hawala can be used at **any phase of the ML cycle**: placement (cash deposited as business proceeds), layering (no paper trail, multiple hawaladars across countries), integration
- FATF has said regulation/supervision of hawala remains a **key challenge** for authorities

### Nonprofit Organizations (NPOs) and Charities

- Terrorists **abuse charitable causes** to trick individuals into contributing
- NPOs are vulnerable because they: enjoy **public trust**, have access to **significant sources of funds**, are often **cash-intensive**, may operate **globally** including in areas close to terrorist activity
- FATF **Recommendation 8** specifically addresses the risk of NPO abuse for TF
- Ways NPOs can be exploited: creation of **sham charities** as fronts for TF, **diversion of funds** from legitimate charities to terrorist purposes, use of legitimate charity operations as **cover** for transferring funds/providing logistical support
- Red flags: large cash deposits, overseas remittances inconsistent with stated purpose, operating in areas with known terrorist presence, lack of transparency in governance

### Emerging Risks: Cryptocurrency, Social Media, and Technology

- **Cryptocurrency** is used by terrorists to raise and move funds -- increasingly sophisticated use of Bitcoin, privacy coins, and decentralized exchanges
- **Social media** used for: fundraising campaigns (sometimes disguised as charitable causes), spreading propaganda to solicit donations, encrypted communication for coordinating financial transfers
- **Crowdfunding** platforms can be exploited to raise funds for terrorist purposes under false pretenses
- Technology enables **faster, more anonymous** transfers that are harder to detect
- FATF guidance emphasizes applying a **risk-based approach** to virtual assets and VASPs

> **Key Exam Points:**
> - The fundamental difference: ML funds always originate from illegal activity; TF funds can come from legal or illegal sources.
> - TF can involve very small amounts, making detection more difficult than large-scale ML.
> - Hawala operates on trust with no paper trail -- can be used at any ML stage. Know the alternative names (hundi, chiti banking, poey kuan).
> - FATF Recommendation 8 addresses NPO vulnerability to TF abuse.
> - Over half of US individuals investigated for terrorist ties had also engaged in suspected ML.
> - Terrorists use the same methods as money launderers (structuring, wire transfers, prepaid cards, cash smuggling) plus hawala, NPO abuse, and increasingly cryptocurrency and social media.
> - Even legitimate funding sources for terrorism need to be disguised to ensure continued use -- this is the "reverse ML" concept.

## Domain I Exam Strategy Tips

- **Scenario-based questions** are common: you will be given a description of an activity and asked to identify the ML stage, method, red flag, or risk
- **Know the three stages** and be able to classify any activity into one
- **Red flags** are heavily tested -- especially for banks, casinos, insurance, securities, and real estate
- For each industry/sector, know: (1) **why** it is vulnerable to ML, (2) key **methods** used, (3) main **red flags**
- **Terrorist financing vs. ML distinctions** are high-yield -- especially the origin-of-funds difference and the small-amount characteristic
- **TBML methods** are frequently tested -- memorize the six techniques
- **BMPE** is a favorite exam topic -- understand the flow from drug dollars to peso brokers to US goods to pesos back to trafficker
- **Hawala/IVTS** -- know how it works, the alternative names, and why it is attractive to criminals
- **Virtual currency/crypto** -- know User/Exchanger/Administrator classifications and FATF Recommendation 15 (VASPs) and 16 (travel rule)
- **Corporate vehicles** -- distinguish shell vs. shelf companies, know bearer shares risks, understand how trusts are abused
